## Key question 1: What is a price change, mechanically, in a limit order book — and how does CME MBO encode it?

### Takeaway
A mid-price change is nothing more than the best queue on one side reaching zero (by fills or by cancels) or a new limit order being posted inside the spread; trade size per se is a weak cause, the depth sitting at the best price is the strong one. CME MBO (via Databento) exposes every add/modify/cancel/fill at order-ID granularity with FIFO priority, so depletion-by-fill, depletion-by-cancel, inside-spread adds, iceberg refreshes and multi-level sweeps are all directly observable as distinct message patterns inside one matching event delimited by `F_LAST`.

### Cited Findings
- **Large price changes are caused by gaps in the book, not by large trades.** Farmer, Gillemot, Lillo, Mike and Sen ("What really causes large price changes?", LSE on-book data, Quantitative Finance 2004) show that the size of a price change is set by the gap between the best price and the next occupied level, and that the volume of the market order has little to do with the size of the resulting price change; most large returns occur when a modest market order meets a sparse book. — [arXiv cond-mat/0312703](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0312703) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Three order-book events move price; trades are only one of them.** Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (Journal of Financial Econometrics 12(1), 47-88, 2014) define order flow imbalance (OFI) as the signed net change of volume at the best bid minus at the best ask, summing new limit orders, cancellations and market orders at the top of the book; over short intervals price changes are driven mainly by this imbalance, with a linear relation whose slope is inversely proportional to depth. The paper used NYSE TAQ data for 50 U.S. stocks. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1712822); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jfinec:v:12:y:2014:i:1:p:47-88.); [arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6402)
- **Limit orders and cancellations carry impact comparable to market orders; which event type dominates the price change depends on tick size.** Eisler, Bouchaud and Kockelkoren ("The price impact of order book events: market orders, limit orders and cancellations", Quantitative Finance 12(9), 2012; U.S. stocks, 2008) decompose the response function by event type and find that in large-tick (one-tick-spread) names, price changes are predominantly produced by limit-order and cancellation activity emptying or refilling the best queues, whereas in small-tick names market orders that walk the book do more of the work. — [arXiv 0904.0900](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0900) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Average book shape and where depth sits.** Bouchaud, Mézard and Potters ("Statistical properties of stock order books", Paris Bourse, Quantitative Finance 2002) measured the time-averaged book as hump-shaped: average depth is *not* maximal at the best quote but a few ticks away, with a power-law placement of incoming limit orders relative to the opposite best; this is why the best queue is often thin relative to what sits behind it. — [arXiv cond-mat/0203511](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0203511) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Market-order impact is damped by anticorrelated limit-order flow (the "virtual vs actual impact" gap).** Weber and Rosenow ("Order book approach to price impact", Island ECN, Quantitative Finance 2005) show that the impact one would compute by walking the pre-trade book ("virtual impact") is far larger than the realized impact because limit orders arrive in the direction opposite to the trade flow. — [arXiv cond-mat/0311457](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0311457) **[not fetched this session]**
- **CME MBO message semantics as normalized by Databento (GLBX.MDP3).** Databento's GLBX.MDP3 documentation states that CME MDP 3.0 messages carry two nanosecond-precision timestamps and Databento adds its own `ts_recv` capture timestamp; `ts_event` is the matching-engine timestamp. Databento sets the `F_LAST` flag (bit 0x80 = 128) on the last record of each matching event *per instrument* (CME itself flags end-of-event once per message, which may span instruments, via tag 5799-MatchEventIndicator). The docs instruct: when computing the best bid/offer use `F_LAST`; between records lacking it the book is mid-update and the apparent BBO "may already have been traded". Records are sorted in priority order once end-of-event is reached, so publication order reflects queue priority. Snapshot records carry `F_SNAPSHOT` (32) and `F_BAD_TS_RECV` (8) with a placeholder `ts_recv`. — [Databento GLBX.MDP3 feed spec](https://databento.com/docs/venues-and-datasets/glbx-mdp3); [Databento MBO snapshot convention](https://databento.com/docs/conventions/mbo-snapshot); [Databento schemas and conventions](https://databento.com/docs/api-reference-historical/basics/schemas-and-conventions)
- **Upcoming change to `F_LAST` placement (July 2026).** Databento announced that CME records will be published per packet rather than buffered to end-of-event, so the final book-update record will no longer carry `F_LAST`; instead a separate record with `F_LAST` and `action='N'` marks the end of each instrument's event. Any book-reconstruction code keyed on `F_LAST` on a substantive record must be checked against the current docs. — [Databento: upcoming CME normalization changes](https://databento.com/blog/cme-normalization-changes-2026-07)
- **CME's own MBO description.** CME's MBO education/FAQ pages describe Market by Order as the dissemination of every individual order with its queue position/priority, in addition to the aggregated Market by Price book. — [CME: Market by Order (MBO)](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/market-by-order-mbo); [CME MBO FAQ](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/faqs/market-by-order-mbo.html)
- **FIFO matching steps on Globex and how display-quantity (iceberg) orders are matched.** CME's matching-algorithm documentation describes, within a FIFO price level, that the displayed portion of a Display Quantity order is filled first in time priority, then any remainder; after a match event completes the order is refreshed with the lesser of the display quantity or the remaining quantity and its priority is reset to the back of the queue at that price. Fills are allocated according to the working displayed quantity at the order's current timestamp. — [CME Globex Matching Algorithm Steps](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/457218521/CME+Globex+Matching+Algorithm+Steps)
- **Iceberg refresh keeps the same OrderID.** CME documentation notes that when displayed quantity is refreshed on a CME-held iceberg, the refreshed order retains the same OrderID as the original, and the CF2 FAQ notes that DQ orders now receive a single consolidated execution report per match event with no change to matching priority. — [CME CF2 FAQ (Why Consolidate Fills?)](https://www.cmegroup.com/globex/trade-on-cme-globex/cf2-faq.html); [CME iLink Execution Report - Fill Messaging Behavior](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/46175085/iLink+Execution+Report+-+Fill+Messaging+Behavior)
- **Pro-rata venues treat icebergs differently.** Under Pro Rata allocation on Globex, Display Quantity orders participate only on their working displayed quantity (relevant for comparison to FIFO products such as outright energy futures). — [CME Globex Matching Algorithm Steps](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/457218521/CME+Globex+Matching+Algorithm+Steps)
- **Databento Trades schema vs MBO.** Databento's Trades schema is the subset of MBO records with action `T` (the aggressor-side trade summary); the MBO schema itself contains the full action set (add, cancel, modify, clear, trade, fill, none). — [Databento Trades schema](https://databento.com/docs/schemas-and-data-formats/trades); [Databento schemas and conventions](https://databento.com/docs/api-reference-historical/basics/schemas-and-conventions)

### Inferences
- A **sweep** in NG MBO data is one matching event: a single `T` (trade summary, aggressor side, total size) followed by a sequence of `F` fills against resting order IDs at successively worse prices, then `C`/`M` records for resting orders whose quantity went to zero or was reduced, then (post-July-2026) an `N` record with `F_LAST`. The number of distinct fill prices in the event is the sweep depth in ticks; the number of distinct order IDs filled is the queue count consumed. Because records within the event are in priority order, the first `F` at each price is the queue head.
- **A trade moves the quote iff the aggressor's size is at least the displayed size of the best queue it hits** (including any iceberg refreshes that occur *inside* the event — per CME the refresh happens after the match event completes, so a single large aggressor can consume the displayed quantity and continue to the next level while the iceberg refreshes behind it). A trade that does not exhaust the queue changes OFI by its size but leaves the mid unchanged; CKS's linear OFI model treats both identically in expectation, which is why the "depth at the best" is the conversion factor between flow and price.
- The practical taxonomy for the project's tape: (i) **fill-depletion** = queue goes to zero via `F` records in a `T` event; (ii) **cancel-depletion** = queue goes to zero via `C`/`M`-down records with no `T` in the same event; (iii) **inside-spread add** = `A` at a price strictly between the prior best bid and ask (only possible when spread > 1 tick, so rare in a one-tick-spread regime); (iv) **iceberg refresh** = an `A` or `M` with an order ID that was just filled to zero displayed size, at the same price, now at the back of the queue. Farmer et al. (2004) imply (i) and (ii) produce multi-tick moves only when the next level is empty, which should be rare in liquid NG front-month during the day session and common in back months and overnight.
- For hidden/iceberg liquidity, CME has no fully hidden order type; the only concealment is the undisplayed remainder of a Display Quantity order. So "hidden liquidity" on Globex is detectable ex post as repeated same-OrderID refreshes at one price.

## Key question 2: Classic theory — what Kyle, Glosten-Milgrom, inventory models, Hasbrouck and Lee-Ready predict about the price response to a trade

### Takeaway
Adverse-selection theory predicts a linear, permanent price response to signed order flow (Kyle's lambda = inverse depth; Glosten-Milgrom's spread as the cost of trading against informed flow); inventory theory predicts a transient response that mean-reverts as the dealer rebalances; Hasbrouck's VAR separates the two empirically (permanent impact = the limit of the cumulative impulse response), and everything downstream depends on correctly signing trades, which Lee-Ready does with 75-85% accuracy in equities and worse for trades inside the spread.

### Cited Findings
- **Kyle (1985), "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading", Econometrica 53(6), 1315-1335.** A single risk-neutral insider trades against noise traders through a competitive market maker; the equilibrium price rule is linear, p = p0 + lambda * (order flow), with lambda = (1/2) sqrt(Sigma0 / sigma_u^2) in the single-auction model, i.e. market depth (1/lambda) rises with noise-trading variance and falls with the variance of private information. In the continuous-auction limit the insider releases information at a constant rate so price impact per unit flow is constant and depth is constant through the day; all private information is in the price by the end. — [JSTOR 1913210](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1913210) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Glosten and Milgrom (1985), "Bid, ask and transaction prices in a specialist market with heterogeneously informed traders", JFE 14(1), 71-100.** A positive bid-ask spread arises purely from adverse selection even with a risk-neutral, zero-profit, competitive market maker; the ask is E[value | next trade is a buy] and the bid is E[value | next trade is a sell]; transaction prices form a martingale and the price change at a trade equals the market maker's Bayesian update, so each trade has a permanent, non-decaying impact and the spread widens with the fraction of informed traders. — [DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Inventory models (Ho and Stoll 1981; Amihud and Mendelson 1980).** A risk-averse dealer shifts both bid and ask down when long and up when short to induce inventory-reducing flow; the quote midpoint moves with inventory and reverts as inventory reverts, producing *transient* price pressure and negative autocorrelation in price changes (Ho-Stoll: "Optimal dealer pricing under transactions and return uncertainty", JFE 9(1), 47-73; Amihud-Mendelson: "Dealership market: Market-making with inventory", JFE 8(1), 31-53, in which bid and ask are monotone decreasing in the dealer's inventory and there is a preferred inventory position). — [Ho-Stoll DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(81)90020-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(81)90020-9); [Amihud-Mendelson DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(80)90012-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(80)90012-3) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Hasbrouck (1991), "Measuring the Information Content of Stock Trades", Journal of Finance 46(1), 179-207.** A bivariate VAR of signed trades and quote-midpoint revisions on NYSE stocks (1989 sample) in which the permanent (informational) impact of a trade is the long-run cumulative impulse response of the quote to a trade innovation; found that the full impact is not realized at the trade but accumulates over subsequent quote revisions, that impact is concave in trade size, and that the information content of trades is larger for smaller/less liquid firms. — [JSTOR 2328693](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2328693) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Hasbrouck (1995), "One Security, Many Markets: Determining the Contributions to Price Discovery", Journal of Finance 50(4), 1175-1199.** The information share: with cointegrated prices across venues, the share of the variance of the common efficient-price innovation attributable to each venue's innovations (bounds from Cholesky ordering). This is the standard tool for the futures-leads-cash question. — [JSTOR 2329348](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2329348) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Roll (1984), "A Simple Implicit Measure of the Effective Bid-Ask Spread in an Efficient Market", Journal of Finance 39(4), 1127-1139.** If trades bounce randomly between bid and ask, consecutive price changes have negative first-order serial covariance and the effective spread S = 2 sqrt(-Cov(dp_t, dp_t-1)); conversely, bid-ask bounce induces spurious negative autocorrelation in trade-price returns that is not a predictable return. — [JSTOR 2327617](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2327617) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Lee and Ready (1991), "Inferring Trade Direction from Intraday Data", Journal of Finance 46(2), 733-746.** Classify a trade as buyer-initiated if above the prevailing quote midpoint, seller-initiated if below, and use the tick test at the midpoint; recommended comparing to quotes lagged 5 seconds because 1980s NYSE quote reporting ran ahead of trade reporting. — [JSTOR 2328845](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2328845) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Measured accuracy of trade-signing rules.** On Nasdaq with true trade signs, the quote rule, the tick rule and the Lee-Ready rule correctly classify 76.4%, 77.66% and 81.05% of trades respectively, and all rules do poorly on trades executed inside the quotes (Ellis, Michaely and O'Hara, JFQA 2000). — [Cambridge Core abstract](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/accuracy-of-trade-classification-rules-evidence-from-nasdaq/A2CAD2206B562784527548F5283A9ECB)
- **Bulk Volume Classification vs tick rule in futures (conflicting evidence).** Easley, Lopez de Prado and O'Hara report the tick rule correctly classifying 86.43% of E-mini S&P 500 volume but only 78.95% in oil futures and 67.18% in gold futures, while BVC classifies 91% (ES), 91.59% (oil) and 87.36% (gold) depending on bar specification; the authors hold a patent interest in BVC. An independent study using CME BBO data for E-mini S&P 500 found BVC inferior to a standard trade-level tick rule. Chakrabarty, Pascual and Shkilko (JFM 25, 2015, 52-79) run the head-to-head on equities. — [Bulk Classification of Trading Activity](https://www.academia.edu/124868502/Bulk_Classification_of_Trading_Activity); [Andersen-Bondarenko CREATES WP 13-43](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/13/rp13_43.pdf); [Chakrabarty et al. 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v25y2015icp52-79.html)
- **Lee-Ready outperforms the tick rule even in fast markets** on data with true signs ("Trade signing in fast markets", University of Memphis). — [Trade signing in fast markets](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/11705)
- **HFTs trade in the direction of permanent price changes and against transitory pricing errors** (Brogaard, Hendershott, Riordan, "High-Frequency Trading and Price Discovery", RFS 27(8), 2014; Nasdaq HFT dataset, 120 stocks, 2008-2009): HFT marketable orders are positively correlated with the permanent component of the price and negatively with the transitory component, i.e. the Hasbrouck decomposition applied to a trader class. — [DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhu032](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhu032) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Fast trading raises adverse selection by construction** (Biais, Foucault and Moinas, "Equilibrium fast trading", JFE 116(2), 2015): investing in speed lets a trader react to news before others, which increases the adverse-selection cost borne by slower liquidity providers and can lead to over-investment in speed. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2015.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2015.03.004) **[not fetched this session]**

### Inferences
- The two classic families make opposite predictions about **decay**: adverse selection says the post-trade price response is permanent (martingale update), inventory says it is transient (mean-reverts with the dealer's inventory). Hasbrouck's cumulative impulse response is the empirical bridge: the plateau is "information", the overshoot-and-decay is "inventory/liquidity". The propagator literature (Q3) later found that in practice single-trade impact decays as a slow power law, i.e. *mostly transient at the trade level* but with the sum over a correlated sequence of trades producing the diffusive price.
- For Kalshi-vs-NYMEX lead-lag, Hasbrouck's information share is the standard measurement, but it assumes cointegration and identical sampling; with Kalshi's coarse cent grid and NYMEX's 0.001 tick, and with the lag measured in seconds, the raw cross-covariance-over-lag approach the project already uses (`odcore/leadlag.py`) is more honest than a Cholesky-bounded share.
- On CME MBO data the Lee-Ready problem disappears for *trades*: the `T` record carries the aggressor side from the exchange, so signing is exact. The ambiguity moves to **which events to count as "flow"** (adds and cancels have no aggressor; OFI handles this by using the book side rather than an initiator).

### Gaps
- Exact sample windows and point estimates for Hasbrouck (1991) (number of stocks, the size of permanent impact in cents) were not retrievable; the JSTOR page was not fetched.
- I found no source in-session on the Hasbrouck-VAR decay horizon measured specifically on futures; the horizon over which the cumulative impulse response plateaus in modern electronic futures is a gap.

## Key question 3: Empirical regularities — order-flow imbalance, queue imbalance, square-root impact, propagator/long memory, Hawkes self-excitation, latent book, liquidity mirage

### Takeaway
Three robust, replicated facts organize the empirical literature: (1) at the shortest horizons the mid moves linearly with top-of-book order-flow imbalance with slope ~1/depth, and the sign of the next tick is predictable from bid/ask queue imbalance — strongly in large-tick instruments, weakly in small-tick ones; (2) the impact of a metaorder grows as the square root of its size (exponent 0.4-0.6 across equities, futures, options, bitcoin), with transient impact that decays after completion; (3) order flow has long memory (sign autocorrelation decaying as a power law, Hurst ~0.7) yet prices are diffusive because liquidity and the impact propagator adjust — the "efficiency paradox". Order and trade arrivals are strongly self-exciting (Hawkes branching ratio near 1 in E-mini futures), which is the statistical face of the same clustering.

### Cited Findings
- **OFI: linear impact with slope inversely proportional to depth; trades alone explain less.** Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (JFEc 2014; 50 NYSE stocks, TAQ Level-I data, short intervals) find a linear relation between OFI and mid-price change with the price impact coefficient inversely proportional to market depth; combined with a scaling argument the linear model reproduces the observed square-root relation between price moves and traded volume, but they find the volume relation noisier and less robust than the OFI relation. Secondary write-ups note the relation weakens at longer horizons and in stressed conditions. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1712822); [arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6402); [Kukanov slides](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Kukanov.pdf); [quantmemo summary](https://quantmemo.com/writing/paper-cont-kukanov-stoikov-order-flow-imbalance). From memory the paper's sample is April 2010, 10-second windows, with an average R^2 of about 65% **[not fetched this session]**.
- **Multi-level OFI.** Xu, Gould, Howison and others extend OFI to deeper levels ("The Price Impact of Generalized Order Flow Imbalance"), finding additional explanatory power from levels beyond the best in some stocks. — [arXiv 2112.02947](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.02947) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Queue imbalance predicts the next mid move; the effect is strong for large-tick stocks and weak for small-tick stocks.** Gould and Bonart (Market Microstructure and Liquidity 2016; Nasdaq LOBSTER data) define imbalance I = (bid queue - ask queue)/(bid + ask) and fit logistic regressions for the direction of the next mid-price move; large-tick stocks (low price, large relative tick) show a "considerable" improvement over the unconditional classifier and small-tick stocks a "moderate" one; the paper's text reports improvements of roughly 20-30% for large-tick stocks versus about 2-6% for small-tick stocks (a secondary source gives 10-30% for small-tick, so take the paper's own figures). Later papers cite it for the statement that the mid price is not efficient in large-tick books because it ignores volume imbalance, and that imbalance loses predictive power when the tick is small. — [arXiv 1512.03492 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/1512.03492v1); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2702117); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/1512.03492.html); [Bonart-Lillo continuous fundamental price](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.00756)
- **Trade arrival and quote imbalance (Lipton, Pesavento, Sotiropoulos 2013).** Models the bid and ask queue sizes as a diffusion and derives the probability that the ask queue depletes first (next move down) as a function of imbalance, matching an empirically near-monotone up-probability-vs-imbalance curve, and shows trade arrival intensity itself depends on imbalance. — [arXiv 1312.0514](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0514) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Markovian queue models give the same prediction analytically.** Cont and de Larrard ("Price dynamics in a Markovian limit order market", SIAM J. Financial Math 2013; Citigroup 2008 as the large-tick example) derive the probability of an up move given bid and ask queue sizes (an arctan-type formula in the queue ratio) and the diffusive limit; Huang, Lehalle and Rosenbaum ("Simulating and analyzing order book data: the queue-reactive model", JASA 2015; Eurex Bund and DAX futures) estimate order-arrival intensities as functions of queue size in large-tick futures and reproduce the book's stationary shape. — [arXiv 1104.4596](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.4596); [arXiv 1312.0563](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Micro-price.** Stoikov's micro-price is the imbalance- and spread-conditioned expected future mid, which outperforms mid and weighted-mid as a short-horizon fair value, especially in large-tick names. — [SSRN 2970694](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2970694) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Square-root law of metaorder impact in futures.** Toth, Lemperiere, Deremble, de Lataillade, Kockelkoren and Bouchaud ("Anomalous price impact and the critical nature of liquidity in financial markets", PRX 2011) measured CFM's proprietary metaorders on futures over June 2007 - December 2010, nearly 500,000 trades; impact measured as average execution shortfall of a metaorder of size Q follows I = Y sigma sqrt(Q/V) across about two orders of magnitude of Q; a separate exponent near 0.6 describes the pre-completion power-law profile and post-completion decay, not the size exponent. CFM slides note the strict square-root is valid in the large participation-ratio limit. — [square-root law search summary; Bouchaud FIPS 2018 slides](https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~ajacquie/FIPS2018/Slides/LondonFIPS2018%20-%20Bouchaud.pdf); [Bouchaud Substack: The Square-Root Law of Market Impact](https://bouchaud.substack.com/p/the-square-root-law-of-market-impact); [Baruch MFE optimal-execution slides](https://mfe.baruch.cuny.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/Chicago2016OptimalExecution.pdf)
- **Square-root replicates across asset classes.** Bitcoin: a million-metaorder analysis found the impact exponent delta very close to 0.5 (Donier and Bonart). Options: fitted slopes delta ~0.40 (long-term) and 0.43 (short-term), "close to 1/2". Tokyo Stock Exchange: exponent anchored around 1/2 across stocks even when other model exponents vary (per Bouchaud's 2024 overview). — [arXiv 1412.4503](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1412.4503); [arXiv 1602.03043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.03043); [Bouchaud Substack](https://bouchaud.substack.com/p/the-square-root-law-of-market-impact)
- **Beyond the square root: logarithmic fits and participation dependence.** Zarinelli, Treccani, Farmer and Lillo ("Beyond the square root: evidence for logarithmic dependence of market impact on size and participation rate", ANcerno institutional orders, U.S. equities, 2007-2009, millions of metaorders) find a logarithmic law fits the full range better than a pure power, with the effective exponent varying with participation rate; they also study post-completion decay. — [SNS PDF](https://ricerca.sns.it/retrieve/9f07ef9b-0fd2-40f1-900d-a571821f9803/Beyond%20the%20square%20root.pdf)
- **"Two square root laws" and the role of volatility/participation.** A 2023 paper separates the square-root in Q from a square-root in participation and discusses which variables are being held fixed in each. — [arXiv 2311.18283](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.18283) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Almgren et al. direct estimation (Citigroup).** Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann and Li ("Direct estimation of equity market impact", Risk 2005) fit ~700,000 U.S. equity orders (Dec 2001 - Jun 2003) and find temporary impact scaling with participation rate to about the 0.6 power and permanent impact close to linear (about 0.9) in order size. — [Almgren et al. PDF](https://www.cims.nyu.edu/~almgren/papers/costestim.pdf) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Long memory of order flow.** Lillo and Farmer ("The long memory of the efficient market", SNDE 2004; LSE on-book order flow 1999-2002) show the autocorrelation of market-order signs decays as a power law with Hurst exponents roughly 0.6-0.8, persisting for thousands of trades/days, while returns are nearly uncorrelated. — [SFI working paper PDF](http://sfi-edu.s3.amazonaws.com/sfi-edu/production/uploads/sfi-com/dev/uploads/filer/d0/d9/d0d92e50-17b7-4072-98a7-293ca01b8d89/03-12-066.pdf); [Theory for long memory in supply and demand (PRE 2005)](https://journals.aps.org/pre/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevE.71.066122); [FX replication](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/porterm/papers/long-memory-published.pdf)
- **Why order flow is persistent: order splitting, not herding.** Toth, Palit, Lillo and Farmer ("Why is order flow so persistent?", JEDC 2015; LSE data with brokerage codes) attribute the long memory mainly to the splitting of large metaorders by single institutions, with herding contributing little at long lags. — [arXiv 1108.1632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.1632). Sato and Kanazawa later verified the Lillo-Mike-Farmer splitting prediction directly with trader-ID data on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. — [arXiv 2301.13505](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13505) **[not fetched this session]**
- **The efficiency paradox and the propagator model.** Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart ("Fluctuations and response in financial markets: the subtle nature of 'random' price changes", Quantitative Finance 2004; Paris Bourse trade data) show that the random-walk nature of prices results from a delicate interplay between long-range-correlated market orders (which alone would make prices super-diffusive) and mean-reverting limit-order liquidity (sub-diffusive), formalized as price = sum over past trades of a time-decaying propagator G(l) times signed volume; G must decay as a power law with exponent beta ~ (1 - gamma)/2 where gamma is the sign-autocorrelation exponent, so that single-trade impact is mostly transient (beta ~0.4 for gamma ~0.2). Lillo and Farmer instead keep a constant propagator and attribute efficiency to liquidity fluctuations synchronized with the long memory of order signs (asymmetric liquidity). — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Fluctuations-and-Response-in-Financial-Markets:-The-Bouchaud-Gefen/83dcc04f87c6712c1e3bd76f78d3ba043284acd1); [Lillo-Farmer SFI PDF](http://sfi-edu.s3.amazonaws.com/sfi-edu/production/uploads/sfi-com/dev/uploads/filer/d0/d9/d0d92e50-17b7-4072-98a7-293ca01b8d89/03-12-066.pdf); recent follow-up: Bouchaud and Kanazawa, "Why do financial prices exhibit Brownian motion despite predictable order flow?" (2025 preprint, not read).
- **The review that ties it together.** Bouchaud, Farmer and Lillo ("How markets slowly digest changes in supply and demand", Handbook of Financial Markets 2009) review: concave single-trade impact (roughly logarithmic in volume), the long memory/efficiency paradox, the propagator model, and the square-root law. — [CFM PDF](https://www.cfm.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/165-2008-How-markets-slowly-digest-changes-in-supply-and-demand.pdf)
- **Hawkes self-excitation and near-criticality in E-mini futures.** Filimonov and Sornette ("Quantifying reflexivity in financial markets", PRE 2012) estimated the Hawkes branching ratio for E-mini S&P 500 mid-price changes rising from about 0.3 in 1998 to about 0.7 by 2007 using exponential kernels; Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud ("Critical reflexivity in financial markets: a Hawkes process analysis", EPJB 2013; E-mini 1998-2012) show that with power-law kernels the fitted branching ratio sits near 1 (critical) throughout, the exponential-kernel trend being an artefact of kernel misspecification. Bacry and Muzy ("Hawkes model for price and trades high-frequency dynamics", Quantitative Finance 2014; Bund and DAX futures) estimate non-parametric kernels that are power laws spanning many time scales (exponent ~1.15), coupling trades and mid-price jumps; Bacry, Mastromatteo and Muzy ("Hawkes processes in finance", Market Microstructure and Liquidity 2015) review. — [arXiv 1201.3572](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.3572); [arXiv 1302.1405](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.1405); [arXiv 1301.1135](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1135); [arXiv 1502.04592](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04592) **[all not fetched this session]**
- **Resiliency measured with Hawkes processes.** Large ("Measuring the resiliency of an electronic limit order book", JFM 10(1), 2007; Barclays on LSE SETS) models ten order-book event types as a mutually exciting Hawkes process and finds that after a large market order depletes the best quote, the book is replenished quickly in only a minority of cases (on the order of 40%), i.e. resiliency is limited and heterogeneous. — [DOI 10.1016/j.finmar.2006.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2006.09.001) **[not fetched this session; the 40% figure is from memory]**
- **Latent order book.** Donier, Bonart, Mastromatteo and Bouchaud ("A fully consistent, minimal model for non-linear market impact", Quantitative Finance 2015) model latent (unrevealed) supply and demand as a reaction-diffusion system whose stationary density vanishes linearly at the price; this makes square-root impact a consequence of linear latent liquidity near the price, predicts the concave impact path during execution and the decay after completion, and reconciles the square root with a linear OFI relation at the top of the visible book. Toth et al. (2011) introduced the same V-shaped latent liquidity argument. — [arXiv 1412.0141](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0141) **[not fetched this session]**; [Baruch slides discussing the models](https://mfe.baruch.cuny.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/09/Chicago2016OptimalExecution.pdf)
- **Liquidity mirage across venues.** Dobrev and Schaumburg (Federal Reserve; "High-Frequency Cross-Market Trading: Model Free Measurement and Applications", and the Liberty Street Economics post "The Liquidity Mirage", 2015) show that displayed depth across distinct liquidity pools conveys a misleading impression of aggregate liquidity because market participants respond to market activity itself; quote modifications and cancellations by high-frequency market makers, rather than pre-emptive aggressive trading, are the important contributor; applications to U.S. Treasury (cash vs futures) and equity (ES/SPY) markets. — [Liberty Street Economics: The Liquidity Mirage](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2015/10/the-liquidity-mirage); [Dobrev-Schaumburg paper (Atlanta Fed 2018)](https://www.atlantafed.org/-/media/documents/news/conferences/2018/1018-financial-stability-implications-of-new-technology/papers/dobrev-schaumburg_high-frequency-cross-market-trading.pdf); [OFR WP 19-04 cross-asset order flow](https://www.financialresearch.gov/working-papers/files/OFRwp-19_04_cross-asset-market-order-flow-liquidity-and-price-discovery.pdf); [ESMA WP 4-2020 HFT and ghost liquidity](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma_wp_4_2020_hft_and_ghost_liquidity.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Tick-size dependence is the single most important moderator in this literature.** Queue imbalance is a near-deterministic next-tick predictor in one-tick-spread books and nearly useless in small-tick books; the mechanism is that in a large-tick book the only way the mid can move is a queue depleting, so the ratio of queue sizes *is* the race. NG at 0.001 with a front-month price around 3 dollars has a relative tick of about 3 bps; whether NG trades as "large tick" (spread pinned at one tick most of the time, per the Dayri-Rosenbaum definition) is an empirical question the project can settle in one pass over its MBP-10 data, and the answer determines whether Gould-Bonart-style imbalance should be expected to work.
- The square-root law concerns *metaorders* (one trader's sequence of child orders) and is not visible in anonymous public data without a proxy; the public-data analogue is the CKS linear OFI relation at short horizons, which CKS themselves show aggregates into the square-root relation between |price change| and volume. For the NG program this means: do not fit |dp| vs volume and call it impact; fit dp vs OFI with depth as the normalizer, per event.
- The efficiency paradox tells you what the Kalshi-lag edge is *not*: it is not "order flow is persistent so price will keep going", because the propagator/liquidity response already cancels that on the venue where the flow occurs. The exploitable asymmetry is cross-venue (NYMEX reprices, Kalshi has not yet), which is the liquidity-mirage/fragmentation channel (Dobrev-Schaumburg; Menkveld-Yueshen in Q4), not the on-venue flow-persistence channel.
- Near-critical Hawkes branching (most events are triggered by prior events, not by news) is the quantitative statement behind "an extreme rate is a LEAD": an activity burst is itself the most likely precursor of more activity, so event-rate spikes are informative about the *timing* of moves independent of direction.

## Key question 4: Liquidity dynamics — resilience after a sweep, cancellation-led thinning vs fill-led depletion, market-maker withdrawal and the flash crash, order anticipation and spoofing, iceberg behaviour

### Takeaway
Books refill after an aggressive order on a scale of tens of quote updates (Paris: within about 20 best-limit updates) but only partially and heterogeneously (LSE: quick replenishment in a minority of cases), and the dangerous regime is when depth leaves by cancellation rather than by fills — the mechanism behind the 2010 flash crash, where intermediaries kept trading but shrank their inventory capacity and cross-market arbitrage broke, so a 75,000-contract E-mini sell program met a book that was being withdrawn faster than it was being consumed.

### Cited Findings
- **Resilience after aggressive orders (Paris Bourse).** Degryse, de Jong, van Ravenswaaij and Wuyts ("Aggressive Orders and the Resiliency of a Limit Order Market", Review of Finance 9(2), 201-242, 2005) find persistence in aggressive-order submission, that aggressive orders arrive when spreads and depths are relatively low, that spreads and depths return to their initial levels within 20 best-limit updates after the shock, and that the price impact is partly reversed while prices retain a long-term tendency to return slowly toward the aggressive order's price. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:revfin:v:9:y:2005:i:2:p:201-242.); [UvA repository](https://dare.uva.nl/id/84569e65-bf29-4378-aa86-936157b871c3); [KU Leuven DPS 02.10 working paper](https://feb.kuleuven.be/research/economics/ces/documents/DPS/2002/DPS0210.pdf)
- **Resiliency after effective market orders: spread, depth, intensity.** A 2016 study measures LOB resiliency along three dimensions after market orders that move the quote (spread recovery, depth recovery, event intensity). — [arXiv 1602.00731 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1602.00731)
- **Futures books: depth is reduced by market orders and by cancellations, and much depth sits beyond the best.** Kovacevic ("Depth and spreads in futures markets: relationship with executions, submissions, and cancellations", AUT) finds market orders and limit-order cancellations both negatively impact best depth; a five-deep electronic futures book study (Journal of Futures Markets 35(6), 542-560, 2015) finds a large amount of depth beyond the best level and more concentrated activity in the day than at night; a related study finds an inverse relation between book depth and spread after controls. — [Kovacevic PDF](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/185302/172512-O-Kovacevic-Depth-and-spreads-in-futures-markets-relationship-with-executions,-submissions,-and-cancellations.pdf); [JFM 35(6) 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v35y2015i6p542-560.html); [Depth and spread relation (IJFS 2021)](https://mdpi.com/2227-7072/9/4/60); [Volatility and depth in commodity and FX futures (JRFM 2021)](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/14/11/545)
- **Flash crash: what HFT intermediaries did.** Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi and Tuzun ("The Flash Crash: High-Frequency Trading in an Electronic Market", Journal of Finance 72(3), 967-998, 2017) use CFTC audit-trail data for E-mini S&P 500 futures on May 6, 2010 and the three prior days; the published finding is that the most active non-designated intraday intermediaries (HFTs) kept their trading pattern steady as prices fell; earlier working-paper versions (NBER 2010, CFTC 2014) stated that HFTs did not trigger the crash but exacerbated volatility by demanding immediacy and aggressively removing the last contracts of liquidity. — [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/304244); [Cambridge PDF](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/304244/1/KirilenkoFlashCrash.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/ab_id=1686004); [CFTC OCE 2014 version](https://www.cftc.gov/idc/groups/public/@economicanalysis/documents/file/oce_flashcrash0314.pdf); [NBER 2010 version](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f5268/f5268.pdf). From the paper/CFTC-SEC report as I recall them **[not fetched this session]**: HFTs were about 16 accounts out of more than 15,000 yet roughly a third of volume; they held inventory within roughly +/-3,000 contracts and mean-reverted it within minutes; in the "hot potato" interval 1:45:13-1:45:27 pm about 27,000 contracts changed hands among HFTs while their net position changed by only ~200; the seller was a 75,000-contract (~$4.1 billion) algorithm targeting 9% of the prior minute's volume with no price or time limit; CME Stop Logic paused trading for 5 seconds at 1:45:28.
- **Flash crash: fragmentation and the breakdown of cross-market arbitrage.** Menkveld and Yueshen ("The Flash Crash: A Cautionary Tale About Highly Fragmented Markets", Management Science 65(10), 4470-4488, 2019) show cross-market arbitrage between the E-mini and the SPY/cash market broke down before the crash, so the large seller's liquidity demand hit a single venue's book rather than the consolidated one; Menkveld's companion work documents "warning tremors" — elevated price vibration — before the crash. — [INFORMS DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2018.3040](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3040); [Tinbergen record](https://tinbergen.nl/publication/159174/the-flash-crash-a-cautionary-tale-about-highly-fragmented-markets); [Menkveld blog: prices vibrate before they crash](https://albertjmenkveld.com/blog/2017-05-17-prices-vibrate-before-they-crash/); [NBER w24098](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24098/revisions/w24098.rev1.pdf)
- **HFT liquidity around mini flash events.** An AUT working paper studies HFT trading activity and liquidity dynamics around mini flash crashes (MFCs). — [HFTs and Flash Events](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0020/294311/HFTs_and_Flash_Events_Trading_Activity_and_Liquidity_Dynamics_around_MFCs.pdf)
- **Ghost liquidity / duplicated quotes across venues.** ESMA's 2020 working paper on HFT and "ghost liquidity" and Gresse's cross-venue liquidity-provision work document that a fraction of displayed depth is duplicated across venues and cancelled once one copy executes, overstating aggregate depth. — [ESMA WP 4-2020](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma_wp_4_2020_hft_and_ghost_liquidity.pdf); [Gresse AMF slides](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/private/2022-01/1_Cross-Venue%20Liquidity%20Provision%20%28C.Gresse%29.pdf)
- **Spoofing and layering.** Lee, Eom and Park ("Microstructure-based manipulation: Strategic behavior and performance of spoofing traders", JFM 16(2), 2013; Korea Exchange account-level data) document spoofing orders placed away from the quotes with large size and then cancelled, which induced other traders to move prices and earned the spoofers excess returns. In U.S. futures, Navinder Sarao was charged in 2015 with layering the E-mini S&P book (large sell orders cancelled before execution) on and around May 6, 2010, and Michael Coscia was convicted in 2015 in the first federal spoofing prosecution for CME/ICE futures. — [Lee-Eom-Park DOI 10.1016/j.finmar.2012.05.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2012.05.004); [DOJ: Sarao charged](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/futures-trader-charged-illegally-manipulating-stock-market-contributing-may-2010-market-flash) **[all not fetched this session]**
- **Icebergs: refresh goes to the back of the queue, same OrderID.** On Globex, after a match event the displayed portion is refreshed with min(display qty, remaining qty) and priority resets to the back of the price level; the refreshed order keeps the same OrderID. — [CME Globex Matching Algorithm Steps](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/457218521/CME+Globex+Matching+Algorithm+Steps); [CME CF2 FAQ](https://www.cmegroup.com/globex/trade-on-cme-globex/cf2-faq.html)
- **Icebergs: empirical.** Frey and Sandas ("The Impact of Iceberg Orders in Limit Order Books", Xetra DAX stocks) find icebergs are detected by other traders, who then trade against the hidden volume, and that iceberg executions carry little adverse selection for the submitter; CME's own EBS case study discusses pricing in with minimal signalling and impact. — [Frey-Sandas SSRN 1108485](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1108485) **[not fetched this session]**; [CME EBS case study](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/case-study/case-study-price-into-ebs-market-with-minimal-signaling-and-market-impact.html); [Bookmap iceberg tracker wiki (practitioner)](https://bookmap.com/wiki/Iceberg_Orders_Tracker); [NexusFi on hidden liquidity (practitioner)](https://nexusfi.com/a/market-structure/iceberg-orders-hidden-liquidity)

### Inferences
- **Fill-led vs cancel-led depletion is distinguishable on MBO and has opposite meaning.** Fill-led depletion is demand consuming supply (aggressor present; `T` event); cancel-led depletion is supply withdrawing without a counterparty (`C`/`M`-down with no `T`). The flash-crash literature is, mechanically, the statement that the second type accelerates during stress and that market makers cancel *before* they are hit (Dobrev-Schaumburg's mechanism). A per-event split of best-queue depletion into fill share vs cancel share is therefore a direct stress/withdrawal gauge — this maps onto the project's S92 finding that turning points are liquidity *recruitment* on the far side rather than consumption: recruitment = adds arriving at the far best, consumption = fills.
- Degryse's "20 best-limit updates" is an event-clock number, not a wall-clock one; on NG the equivalent recovery time is minutes overnight and well under a second in the active session. Measure resilience in event time first, then map to wall clock per session-regime cell.
- Iceberg detection on CME MBO is clean: a queue head filled to zero displayed quantity that reappears with the same `order_id` at the same price with a new (last) priority is an iceberg refresh; the count of refreshes times the display size lower-bounds the hidden size. Because refresh happens *after* the match event, a sweep that exceeds the displayed size at a level walks past the iceberg to the next level within the same event, so iceberg presence reduces *subsequent* events' impact rather than the current sweep's.

- I found no peer-reviewed study of spoofing/layering patterns specifically in NYMEX energy MBO data.

## Key question 5: Futures-specific — CME Globex microstructure (large-tick regime, queue-position value, implied orders, Stop Logic, Velocity Logic) and energy books

### Takeaway
Globex outright futures match FIFO, so in one-tick-spread (large-tick) contracts queue position is the scarce resource and most of the "price formation" happens as queue races rather than as spread crossing; Globex also carries exchange-level mechanisms (Stop Logic / Velocity Logic reserved states, price banding) that interrupt the normal book dynamics exactly during fast moves, and implied spread liquidity sits in the outright book without being visible as individual orders. Peer-reviewed microstructure work on NYMEX natural gas books specifically is thin; the depth/spread/volatility relationships are documented on other futures.

### Cited Findings

A 2015 CME Globex notice set production values at two times the greater of the non-reviewable range or the price-banding range for the point value and one second for the time value; a 2013 notice specified that an aggressing order that would trigger it is rejected via an Execution Report-Reject while the market stays open. — [CME Velocity Logic (client wiki)](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/457218368/Velocity+Logic); [CME Globex Notice 2015-08-31](https://www.cmegroup.com/tools-information/lookups/advisories/electronic-trading/20150831.html); [CME Globex Notice 2013-03-11](https://www.cmegroup.com/tools-information/lookups/advisories/electronic-trading/20130311.html); [CME Market Integrity Controls](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/46170793); [CQG introduction to Velocity Logic](https://news.cqg.com/blogs/2023/01/introduction-cme-globex-velocity-logic)

### Inferences
- **Classify NG by regime before applying any imbalance result.** If the front-month NG spread is pinned at 0.001 for most of the active session, NG is a large-tick book and (a) Gould-Bonart/Cont-de Larrard queue-imbalance predictability should be strong at the one-tick horizon, (b) most price changes will be depletion events rather than inside-spread adds, and (c) queue position value is material, so the far-side "recruitment" the project observed is partly a queue-position race. Back months and the overnight session will be small-tick-like (multi-tick spreads), where imbalance predictability collapses and inside-spread adds become a main price-change mechanism. These are per-cell, not pooled, regimes.
- Velocity Logic/Stop Logic reserved states are **exchange-imposed discontinuities** in the tape: during them the book accepts orders but does not match, so OFI accumulates without price response and the first post-reserve event has an anomalously large impact. Any impact regression that spans a reserved state will be contaminated; the Databento halts example shows how to detect them, and the project's settle-window exclusion should be joined by a reserved-state exclusion.
- Implied liquidity from calendar spreads is part of the NG outright MBP depth but not of the MBO order stream, so OFI computed from MBO adds/cancels will miss implied-depth changes that MBP-10 shows; reconcile the two before trusting either as "the book".

## Key question 6: Measurement pitfalls — bid-ask bounce, clock choice, stale quotes, overlapping windows, side classification, look-ahead

### Takeaway
Most spurious "edges" in order-book research come from five sources: bid-ask bounce masquerading as mean reversion in trade prices, using the wrong clock (receive vs matching-engine time) or reading the book mid-event, overlapping windows inflating sample size, mis-signed trades, and defining a signal by information that only exists after the outcome. On CME MBO data, trade signing is exact and both timestamps are available, so the remaining traps are book-state timing (`F_LAST`), event-vs-wall clock, and look-ahead in the signal definition.

### Cited Findings
- **Bid-ask bounce.** Roll (1984) shows random alternation between bid and ask produces negative first-order autocovariance in transaction-price changes equal to -S^2/4; this is a measurement artefact, not a return, and any analysis on trade prices (rather than mid or micro-price) must remove it. — [JSTOR 2327617](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2327617) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Clock choice and mid-event book states (CME via Databento).** `ts_event` is the matching-engine timestamp and `ts_recv` the capture-server timestamp; the BBO must be computed only on records flagged `F_LAST`, because between records in one event the book is still updating and the apparent BBO may already have been traded; snapshot records carry a placeholder `ts_recv` and `F_BAD_TS_RECV`. The July 2026 normalization change moves `F_LAST` to a separate `action='N'` record. — [Databento GLBX.MDP3](https://databento.com/docs/venues-and-datasets/glbx-mdp3); [Databento MBO snapshot](https://databento.com/docs/conventions/mbo-snapshot); [Databento normalization change](https://databento.com/blog/cme-normalization-changes-2026-07)
- **Quote lag conventions are venue- and era-specific.** Lee and Ready's 5-second quote lag was a fix for 1980s NYSE reporting; applying it blindly to modern electronic markets degrades classification, and on Nasdaq with true signs Lee-Ready reached 81.05% accuracy with inside-the-quote trades the main failure mode. — [Cambridge Core (Ellis-Michaely-O'Hara)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/accuracy-of-trade-classification-rules-evidence-from-nasdaq/A2CAD2206B562784527548F5283A9ECB); [Trade-sign classification playbook (practitioner, low reliability)](https://velobot-wiki.pages.dev/article/finance/trade-sign-classification-tick-rule-quote-rule-lee-ready-bvc-playbook/)
- **Bulk classification disputes.** The disagreement between Easley-Lopez de Prado-O'Hara (BVC superior) and Andersen-Bondarenko (tick rule superior on CME E-mini BBO data) shows that side-classification results depend on the data (bar-aggregated vs trade-level) and on who holds the ground truth. — [Andersen-Bondarenko](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/13/rp13_43.pdf); [Bulk Classification of Trading Activity](https://www.academia.edu/124868502/Bulk_Classification_of_Trading_Activity)
- **Horizon dependence of OFI results.** Secondary summaries of CKS note the OFI-price relation weakens at longer horizons and in stressed conditions — a short-horizon result should not be extrapolated. — [quantmemo summary](https://quantmemo.com/writing/paper-cont-kukanov-stoikov-order-flow-imbalance); [Micro Alphas OFI note](https://microalphas.com/order-flow-imbalance/)
- **Kernel misspecification can manufacture a trend.** Hardiman, Bercot and Bouchaud show the apparent rise of the Hawkes branching ratio over 1998-2007 in E-mini data is an artefact of fitting exponential kernels to power-law-kernel data; the model choice created the "finding". — [arXiv 1302.1405](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.1405) **[not fetched this session]**
- **Event time vs calendar time changes the memory you see.** A 2026 preprint on event-time order-flow memory and operational-time impact discusses how subordinating observables to event time alters measured persistence and impact. — [arXiv 2609.13715](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.13715) **[not fetched this session]**

### Inferences
- **Overlapping windows.** When a forward return over k events/seconds is regressed on a signal sampled every event, consecutive observations share k-1 of their k increments; the effective sample size is roughly N/k and naive standard errors are overstated by about sqrt(k). Use non-overlapping windows or Hansen-Hodrick/Newey-West standard errors with lag >= k; per-event analysis (one row per event, as the project does) is the cleanest fix. (Methodological inference; no single source fetched.)
- **Look-ahead through the signal definition.** Signals such as "the leg's imbalance" or "the metaorder's size" are often computed over an interval whose endpoint is defined by the outcome (the leg ends when price reverses; the metaorder ends when the trader stops). Any feature that uses the full interval is conditioned on the future; only features computed up to a fixed pre-entry cutoff are admissible — this is the leakage gate the project already mandates (`odcore/leakage.py`), and the square-root-law literature's "impact at completion" is the canonical example of a quantity that is only known ex post.
- **Stale-quote problem on CME is a book-state problem, not a reporting-lag problem.** With `ts_event` and `F_LAST` there is no reporting lag to correct; the error is instead reading the BBO from a partial event. On the receive side, `ts_recv - ts_event` (the feed-to-capture latency) is the right variable to check for clock skew, and the only clock that is meaningful for cross-venue lag measurement (NYMEX vs Kalshi) is a common receive clock, since Kalshi has no matching-engine timestamp comparable to CME's.
- **Pooling across tick regimes is itself a pitfall**: a sample that mixes one-tick-spread and multi-tick-spread states will show a diluted imbalance effect that "fails" on average while being strong in one cell, exactly the Gould-Bonart large/small-tick split; the project's per-cell rule already guards this.
