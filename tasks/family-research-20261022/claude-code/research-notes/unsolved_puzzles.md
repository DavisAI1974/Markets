# Unsolved Puzzles and Unexplained Regularities in Financial and Commodity Markets

Scope note for the report writer: these notes catalog the major open problems about *why prices move the way they do*, with (a) the regularity, (b) competing explanations, (c) evidence for/against, (d) the discriminating observation, and (e) a consensus marker. Consensus markers used: **SETTLED-REGULARITY / OPEN-MECHANISM** (the fact is agreed, the cause is not), **CONTESTED-FACT** (even the regularity is disputed), **PARTIAL-CONSENSUS** (one mechanism dominates but is not treated as proven), **OPEN**. Research constraints: web fetch of primary PDFs (arxiv.org, nber.org, cftc.gov, substack, columbia.edu, ucsd.edu) was blocked by the session's network allowlist, and the per-turn search budget ran out before four planned queries (momentum risk-vs-behavior reviews; oil-price jump/news event studies; Lux-Marchesi agent-based volatility clustering vs mixture-of-distributions; Prokopczuk NG announcement-day drift mechanism). Everything below is drawn from search-result summaries of abstracts and secondary pages; where only abstracts were visible that is flagged. Unsourced background knowledge is confined to the Inferences and Gaps sections and labelled as such.

## KQ1. Statistical "stylized facts" without agreed causes

### Takeaway
The stylized facts (fat tails with tail exponent ~3, volatility clustering/long memory, leverage effect, intermittency/multifractality, rough volatility, aggregational Gaussianity) are well-established as regularities but every proposed *mechanism* is contested; the two most active live disputes are (i) whether large-trader size/order-splitting generates the power-law tails (Gabaix et al. vs Farmer-Lillo), and (ii) whether "rough volatility" is a property of volatility or an artefact of estimating it from noisy high-frequency data (Gatheral-Jaisson-Rosenbaum vs Cont-Das).

### Cited Findings

**The catalogue itself (Cont 2001) and its modern re-test**
- Cont (2001, *Quantitative Finance* 1(2):223-236) presents stylized facts across many markets covering distributional properties, tail properties and extreme fluctuations, pathwise regularity, and linear/nonlinear dependence in time and across stocks; he treats them as constraints any stochastic model must satisfy — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:1:y:2001:i:2:p:223-236); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/taf/quantf/v1y2001i2p223-236.html)
- The 11 facts were drawn from different studies, so no single asset was used for all 11 — [Ratliff-Crain et al. 2023, arXiv 2311.07738](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07738)
- Ratliff-Crain et al. (2023) retested Cont's facts on intraday returns of Dow 30 stocks (Oct 2018-Mar 2019) and found conclusive evidence for eight of the original facts and no support for the remaining three — [arXiv 2311.07738](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.07738v2); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/arxpapers/2311.07738.htm)

**Fat tails / power laws (the "cubic law")**
- Gabaix, Gopikrishnan, Plerou, Stanley (*Nature* 423:267-270, 2003) start from the empirical size distribution of large participants (mutual funds) and show power laws in returns, volumes and number of trades arise when trading is performed optimally; the model "rests on a hypothesis that large market movements come from large institutions' trades" — [Gabaix Harvard page](https://xgabaix.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/theory-power-law-distributions-financial-market-fluctuations); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v423y2003i6937d10.1038_nature01624.html); [MIT DSpace PDF](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a431e556-98f8-439b-922e-996a9489edf1/content)
- The targets are the cubic law of returns (exponent 3), the half-cubic law of volumes (exponent 3/2), and an approximate cubic law of number of trades; exponents are similar across market types/sizes, trends and countries, taken as a sign of a generic mechanism — [UVM mirror of Gabaix 2003a](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/everything/gabaix2003a.pdf); [CXO summary](https://cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/classic-research-explaining-large-stock-market-fluctuations)
- Gabaix: "the forces that give rise to the power laws of stock market fluctuations are extremely robust... such crashes would be very, very hard to prevent" — [MIT News 2003](https://news.mit.edu/2003/powerlaw)
- **Against (Farmer & Lillo 2003-04):** LSE volumes showed no power-law tail; the square-root impact law implies a price exponent too large for LSE/NYSE stocks; "the long-memory nature of order flow invalidates the statistical analysis of market impact"; for both LSE and NYSE "the average market impact function grows much slower than a square root law"; conclusion: "volume fluctuations do not determine the power law tail of price returns" — [arXiv cond-mat/0309416](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0309416); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:arx:papers:cond-mat/0309416)
- **Reply (Plerou, Gopikrishnan, Gabaix, Stanley 2004):** volume distribution decays as a power law with exponent ~3/2 across 262 of the largest LSE stocks; large orders are split, so Farmer-Lillo's procedures give a downward bias on the volume exponent — [arXiv cond-mat/0403067](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0403067); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0403067)
- **Farmer & Lillo follow-up ("There's more to volatility than volume", 2005):** the distribution of returns conditioned on volume or transaction frequency held constant is similar to that in real time, i.e. volume alone is not the principal cause of heavy tails — [ar5iv physics/0510007](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/physics/0510007)
- An alternative route to the tails is self-excitation: Filimonov & Sornette (2012) propose the Hawkes "branching ratio" n as a direct measure of endogeneity; their E-mini S&P analysis 1998-2010 indicated rising reflexivity linked to flash-crash risk — [arXiv 1201.3572](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1201.3572)
- Hardiman, Bercot & Bouchaud (2013), same data but power-law memory kernel: markets "are and have always been close to criticality" (no rising trend) — [arXiv 1302.1405](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1302.1405)
- Filimonov & Sornette (2013) "Apparent criticality": regime shifts in Hawkes parameters and/or the generating process cause "a significant upward bias in the estimation of the branching ratio," so near-criticality "cannot be supported" — [arXiv 1308.6756](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6756)
- Hardiman & Bouchaud (2014) non-parametric branching-ratio estimator: S&P 500 futures show "a stable level of reflexivity, close to the criticality" over the last ten years — [Hawkes processes in finance review, arXiv 1502.04592](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.04592) (review covering the estimation pitfalls incl. exponential-kernel bias)
- A 2020 Bitcoin mid-price study finds "a level of criticality similar to fiat-currency markets" — [European Journal of Finance 2020](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1351847X.2020.1791925)

**Long memory of volatility vs. rough volatility**
- Gatheral, Jaisson, Rosenbaum ("Volatility is rough", arXiv 2014, *Quant. Finance* 2018): log-volatility "behaves essentially as a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H of order 0.1"; "although volatility is not long memory in the RFSV model, classical statistical procedures aiming at detecting volatility persistence tend to conclude the presence of long memory in data generated from it"; they give "a quantitative market microstructure-based foundation... relating the roughness of volatility to high frequency trading and order splitting" and claim improved RV forecasts — [arXiv 1410.3394](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3394); [EconPapers QF 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:18:y:2018:i:6:p:933-949)
- **Against:** Cont & Das ("Rough volatility: fact or artefact?", arXiv 2203.13820, 2022/rev. 2023): in simulations where instantaneous volatility is diffusive, realized volatility still looks rough with apparent H well below 0.5 — "irrespective of the roughness of the spot volatility process, realized volatility always exhibits 'rough' behaviour"; "the origin of the roughness observed in realized volatility time-series lies in the microstructure noise rather than the volatility process itself" — [arXiv 2203.13820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13820); [Imperial seminar](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/events/147161/purba-das-rough-volatility-fact-or-artefact/)
- Search found no direct published rebuttal of Cont-Das — see Gaps.

**Leverage effect**
- Bouchaud, Matacz, Potters (PRL 2001): negative correlation between past returns and future volatility; for individual stocks "moderate and decays exponentially over 50 days", for indices "much stronger but decays faster"; for indices "a specific market panic phenomenon seems to be necessary to account for the observed amplitude" — [arXiv cond-mat/0101120](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0101120); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sfi:sfiwpa:0101120)
- Companion "retarded volatility model" (Bouchaud & Potters, Physica A 299:60-70): for single stocks the leverage correlation "can be rationalized in terms of a new 'retarded' model which interpolates between a purely additive and a purely multiplicative stochastic process" — [EconPapers Physica A](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:phsmap:v:299:y:2001:i:1:p:60-70)
- Search did not surface the classic firm-leverage (Black/Christie) vs volatility-feedback (Campbell-Hentschel/French-Schwert-Stambaugh) literature — see Gaps.

**Intermittency / multifractality**
- The Multifractal Random Walk (Bacry-Delour-Muzy) "is able to reproduce most of recent empirical findings concerning financial time-series: no correlation" in returns with persistent volatility correlation — [alphaxiv cond-mat/0005400](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0005400.md); Calvet-Fisher's Markov-switching multifractal (MSM) is the main rival with developed ML inference — [arXiv 1202.5376](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.5376)
- **Against:** evidence for multifractal scaling "has relied on informal tests that may be unable to reliably distinguish multifractal" from unifractal processes; some return series' scaling "may be inconsistent with purely multifractal processes" — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/411635/1/final_accepted_version.pdf)
- "Many authors have reported to find no evidence of multifractal scaling in different data sets and report spurious multiscaling for different model types" — [Osijek math paper](https://www.mathos.unios.hr/wp-content/uploads/uploads/117.pdf)
- Equity returns show "time varying multifractal properties," challenging a single fixed intermittency parameter — [Non-stationary multifractality, arXiv 1212.3195](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1212.3195)

**Volatility clustering mechanisms**
- Gatheral et al. tie roughness/persistence to HFT and order splitting (above). The Hawkes near-criticality literature (above) offers self-excitation as a mechanism. Toth-Palit-Lillo-Farmer show order-sign persistence is "overwhelmingly due to splitting rather than herding" at horizons under a few hours — [arXiv 1108.1632](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.1632v1) (relevant because splitting-driven persistence is the microstructural input to the GJR roughness argument).
- The search budget expired before the agent-based (Lux-Marchesi) and mixture-of-distributions/news-arrival strands could be retrieved — see Gaps.

### Inferences
- Classification by mechanistic status (my synthesis of the above): **fat tails** — SETTLED-REGULARITY / OPEN-MECHANISM, with two camps (large-trader size + concave impact vs. endogenous self-excitation/liquidity criticality; Farmer-Lillo show the volume channel alone fails). **Volatility clustering / long memory** — regularity settled; whether it is true long memory or a roughness/self-excitation artefact is OPEN and the two readings make different forecasting claims. **Rough volatility** — CONTESTED-FACT at the level of the regularity itself (Cont-Das). **Leverage effect** — regularity settled; index-level amplitude exceeds what single-stock mechanisms produce, indicating a market-wide "panic"/feedback component not accounted for by firm leverage. **Multifractality** — CONTESTED-FACT (informal scaling tests; non-stationarity). **Aggregational Gaussianity** — one of Cont's facts, generally uncontroversial as a regularity (no source retrieved disputes it); mechanism is essentially a CLT-with-dependence question and attracts little dispute.
- Discriminating observations: (1) for tails — condition return tails on volume/trade frequency (Farmer-Lillo design) across more markets and eras, and test whether tail exponents move with the concentration of institutional ownership as Gabaix predicts; (2) for roughness — apply Cont-Das's normalized p-th variation estimator to option-implied forward variance or to volatility proxies with independent noise structure; if roughness persists where microstructure noise is absent, GJR wins; (3) for Hawkes criticality — the model-free branching-ratio estimators must agree across kernels and sub-samples; divergence across kernel choice is itself evidence for the Filimonov-Sornette misspecification critique.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Cont (2001), Gabaix et al. (2003), GJR (2018) and Cont-Das were not accessible (arxiv.org/nber.org blocked); numbers are from abstracts and secondary summaries.
- No published rebuttal of Cont-Das was found; the status of rough-volatility as "fact" is therefore unresolved in these notes.
- The classic leverage-effect literature (firm-leverage hypothesis vs volatility feedback) was not retrieved; search surfaced only the econophysics strand.
- Agent-based explanations of volatility clustering (Lux-Marchesi etc.) and the mixture-of-distributions/news-arrival hypothesis were not retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- No source was found comparing how well Hawkes self-excitation vs heterogeneous-agent models reproduce the full set of Cont's facts simultaneously.

## KQ2. Excess volatility and the "no-news" problem (equities and energy)

### Takeaway
Shiller's finding that prices move far more than subsequent fundamentals justify survived its main econometric critiques (Kleidon, Marsh-Merton) in the judgment of most reviewers; the related Cutler-Poterba-Summers and Fair results — most large moves have no identifiable news — have been replicated at jump resolution by Joulin et al. (2008) and Ait-Sahalia & Li (2024), and extended to commodities via Pindyck-Rotemberg's "excess co-movement." No consensus mechanism exists; the leading candidates are (a) time-varying discount rates, (b) flow/inelastic-demand shocks, and (c) endogenous liquidity dynamics.

### Cited Findings

**Equities: excess volatility**
- Kleidon (1986) argued Shiller used ex-post dividends not known when prices were set and used Monte Carlo simulation "to show that the valuation model holds and is consistent with market efficiency"; variance-bound tests are "significantly biased if dividends contain a quantitatively important 'unit root'" — [McMaster working paper](https://profs.degroote.mcmaster.ca/ads/balvers/MFHEANEY.pdf); [variance-bound critique page](https://sites.google.com/site/davesmant/monetary-economics/variance-bound)
- Marsh & Merton (1986): dividend smoothing can make prices nonstationary so finite samples look excessively volatile; "if dividends follow a random walk then one can potentially reconcile Shiller's results with an efficient markets model" — [FRBSF WP 10-24](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp10-24bk.pdf)
- Correcting these problems "did not eliminate the appearance of excess volatility"; Shiller judged Kleidon-type considerations "too small to quantitatively account for excess variance"; later evidence of a mean-reverting component in dividends weakened the unit-root argument — [Shiller, From Efficient Markets to Behavioral Finance (Berkeley mirror)](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~craine/EconH195/Fall_16/webpage/Shiller_Efficient%20Mkts.pdf)
- West (1988) review "concludes that there is excess volatility in the stock market"; other critics include Shea (1989) on statistical tests and Ackert & Smith (1993) on dividend definitions — [CORE download](https://core.ac.uk/download/45670397.pdf)

**Equities: large moves without news**
- Cutler, Poterba, Summers (1989, *JPM*; NBER WP 2538): "difficult to explain more than one third of the return variance" from macro news; moves around major political/world events "were relatively small"; large moves often occur on days without identifiable news — [NBER w2538](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2538); [MIT DSpace PDF](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/64351/whatmovesstockpr00cutl.pdf); partly a response to Roll's finding that only about a third of index variation is attributable to economic influences — [Cutler Harvard page](https://cutler.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/what-moves-stock-prices)
- Fair (2002, *J. Business* 75(4):713-732) used S&P 500 futures tick data and newswire searches to link news to large 1-5 minute moves: "Sixty-nine events that led to large stock price changes are identified between 1982 and 1999, 53 of which are directly or indirectly related to monetary policy"; "many large stock price changes have no events associated with them" — [Fair bepress](https://works.bepress.com/ray_fair/10); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jnlbus:v:75:y:2002:i:4:p:713-732)
- Joulin, Lefevre, Grunberg, Bouchaud (2008, arXiv 0803.1769): cross-correlating high-frequency returns with news feeds, "most large jumps... are not related to any broadcasted news"; "direct evidence that large transaction volumes are not responsible for large price jumps"; "most price jumps are induced by order flow fluctuations close to the point of vanishing liquidity" — [arXiv 0803.1769](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.1769.pdf); [CFM page](https://www.cfm.com/stock-price-jumps-news-and-volume-play-a-minor-role/); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0803.1769)
- Later Bouchaud-group work: idiosyncratic jumps occur mostly (~95%) without news — [arXiv 2106.07040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07040v1); a 2025 wavelet-based classification of price jumps — [Bouchaud Substack](https://bouchaud.substack.com/p/riding-wavelets-a-new-method-to-classify); Risk.net coverage "Why do prices jump?" — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/node/7963657) and "Market's mystery jumps might be predictable after all" — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/ja/node/7847051)
- Ait-Sahalia & Li, "So Many Jumps, So Few News" (NBER WP 32746, July 2024): "Most relevant news, both idiosyncratic and systematic, lead quickly to price jumps, as market efficiency suggests they should" but "the vast majority of price jumps do not have identifiable public news"; insider trading considered unlikely — "A firm is unlikely to generate significant information, private or public, at a pace consistent with the high rate of incidence of jumps"; "microstructure-driven variables have only limited predictive power to help distinguish between jumps with and without news" — [NBER w32746](https://nber.org/papers/w32746); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/32746.html)
- ETF jump study: 43% of detected jumps related to macro news, 57% not — [EFMA 2016 paper](https://efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2016-Switzerland/papers/EFMA2016_0011_fullpaper.pdf)

**Commodities: excess volatility / co-movement**
- Pindyck & Rotemberg (NBER WP 2671, 1988; *Economic Journal* 100(403):1173-89, 1990): co-movement among unrelated raw commodities is larger than macro variables (inflation, industrial production, interest rates, exchange rates) can account for, even after allowing for latent common shocks; result "rejects the standard competitive model of commodity price formation with storage" — [NBER w2671](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2671.pdf); [EconPapers EJ](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecj:econjl:v:100:y:1990:i:403:p:1173-89); data at [ICPSR 1032](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/1032/CoreTrustSeal)
- Deaton & Laroque (1992, *REStud* 59:1-23): competitive storage model "explains the skewness, and the existence of rare but violent explosions in prices" but "does not yield a fully satisfactory explanation for the high autocorrelation observed in the data"; 1996 follow-up with autocorrelated shocks still "did not find parameter values whose simulations reproduced the high levels of positive autocorrelation" — "speculative storage by itself is not sufficient" — [Princeton PDF](https://www.princeton.edu/~deaton/downloads/Competitive_Storage_and_Commodity_Price_Dynamics.pdf); [EconPapers REStud](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:59:y:1992:i:1:p:1-23.)
- **Rebuttal:** Cafiero et al. (2011, *J. Econometrics* 162:44-54): a properly parameterized numerical storage model "can generate the high levels of serial correlation observed in commodity prices"; estimated on seven commodities, "observed magnitudes of serial correlation of price match those implied by the estimated model" — [EconPapers J.Econometrics](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:162:y:2011:i:1:p:44-54)
- Fed IFDP 1065: within the Deaton-Laroque framework, increased price volatility "is likely attributable to changes in the underlying shock processes rather than interest rates" — [Fed IFDP 1065](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2012/1065/revision/ifdp1065r.htm); Mitra & Boussard add an endogenous cobweb mechanism producing positive skewness and autocorrelation — [Fordham DP 2008-11](https://archive.fordham.edu/ECONOMICS_RESEARCH/PAPERS/dp2008_11_mitra_boussard.pdf)
- Roll (1984, *AER*) on frozen orange juice: weather is the most identifiable factor yet "weather surprises explain only a small fraction of the observed variability in futures prices"; also a significant relation between OJ returns and *subsequent* NWS temperature-forecast errors — [Caltech record](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20190501-112416217); [NBER w9515 citing](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9515.pdf)
- Boudoukh et al. (2007) revisited Roll and found FCOJ prices do react significantly to temperature once the state of the world (freeze-threshold nonlinearity) is conditioned on; an NYU paper argues "there is absolutely no puzzle concerning the relation between FCOJ returns and temperature" — [NYU archive 02-43](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/26487/2/02-43.pdf); [NYU S-DRP-03-05](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/26821/2/S-DRP-03-05.pdf)

**Candidate mechanisms**
- Flow/inelastic demand: Gabaix & Koijen estimate "investing $1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5" and that aggregate demand elasticity is small because institutions hold mandates (fixed equity shares), so "flows in and out of the stock market have large impacts on prices"; they stress it "remains a hypothesis" — [NBER w28967](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28967); [CEPR DP16290](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP16290.pdf)
- Endogenous liquidity: Joulin et al.'s "vanishing liquidity" (above); Bouchaud's Latent Liquidity Model is "presented as connecting the intraday square-root law to longer-run findings such as Gabaix and Koijen's" — [Scientia profile](https://www.scientia.global/professor-jean-philippe-bouchaud-the-inelastic-market-hypothesis-explaining-the-origins-of-financial-fluctuations/)

### Inferences
- Marker: **excess volatility (equity)** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS that the regularity is real (West 1988, Shiller); mechanism OPEN among discount-rate variation, flows, and behavioral/liquidity channels. **No-news jumps** — SETTLED-REGULARITY (replicated 1989, 2002, 2008, 2024 at daily, 5-minute and jump resolution) / OPEN-MECHANISM; the 2024 NBER paper explicitly rules out insider trading as the bulk explanation and finds microstructure variables weakly discriminating, which narrows the field toward flow- and liquidity-driven endogeneity but does not prove it.
- The commodity analogue is two-layered: Pindyck-Rotemberg excess co-movement (OPEN; the storage model is rejected) and Deaton-Laroque autocorrelation (CONTESTED — Cafiero et al. say it is a parameterization problem). Roll's OJ puzzle is the cleanest case where a "weather explains little" result was substantially dissolved by conditioning on the state (nonlinear freeze threshold) — a template for how NG "weather vs price" questions should be posed.
- Discriminating observations: (a) if flows drive excess volatility, exogenous flow shocks (index reconstitutions, mandated rebalancing, retail inflows) should produce persistent price moves with no reversal and no subsequent cash-flow news; if liquidity endogeneity drives it, moves should cluster where displayed depth is thin and partly revert; (b) for no-news jumps, the Joulin et al. signature — volatility decays differently after endogenous vs news jumps — is a testable fingerprint on any tape (the specific decay exponents are in the full paper, not retrievable here); (c) for commodities, Cafiero-style estimation on energy (not just agriculture) would tell whether storage alone can reproduce NG/CL autocorrelation and volatility.

### Gaps
- No energy-market equivalent of Cutler-Poterba-Summers/Fair (large CL/NG daily moves vs identified news) was retrieved; the search budget expired on that query. The closest found is Roll (1984) on OJ and Pindyck-Rotemberg on cross-commodity co-movement.
- Specific decay-exponent numbers from Joulin et al. (volatility relaxation after endogenous vs exogenous jumps) could not be extracted (arxiv blocked).
- Shiller (1981) original and West (1988) full texts not retrieved; the variance-bound critiques are summarized from secondary sources.
- No commodity-specific test of the inelastic markets hypothesis (price multiplier per dollar of flow in futures) was found.

## KQ3. Microstructure puzzles

### Takeaway
The best-documented microstructure puzzles — long-memory order flow coexisting with near-martingale prices, the near-universal concave (square-root) impact of metaorders, the small displayed-vs-latent liquidity, sudden liquidity evaporation, and endogenous jumps — are increasingly treated by the econophysics school as one coupled phenomenon ("liquidity is critically small near the price, so prices are diffusive only through a fine balance"), but this is a research programme rather than a consensus; the mainstream alternative (Kyle-Obizhaeva invariance) reaches the square-root law from a different direction and disagrees on universality.

### Cited Findings

**Long memory of order flow vs. efficiency ("efficiency paradox")**
- Bouchaud, Farmer, Lillo review ("How markets slowly digest changes in supply and demand", arXiv 0809.0822): large orders "can only be traded incrementally, over periods of time as long as months," making order flow a highly persistent long-memory process; "long memory must coexist with market efficiency (at least in a statistical sense)," which "forces asymmetric liquidity adjustments between buyers and sellers" — [arXiv 0809.0822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.0822v1.pdf); [arxiv-vanity](https://www.arxiv-vanity.com/papers/0809.0822/)
- Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters, Wyart (*QF* 2004, 4(2):176-190), Paris data: random-walk prices arise from a balance between persistent, correlated market orders (pushing toward super-diffusion) and mean-reverting limit orders (toward sub-diffusion); the market sits "at a critical point, where prices are purely diffusive and the average response function is nearly constant"; "the information carried by an individual trade is on average very small, and... the fraction of truly informed market orders is quite small" — [arXiv cond-mat/0307332](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0307332v2); [EconPapers QF](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:4:y:2004:i:2:p:176-190)
- Toth, Palit, Lillo, Farmer ("Why is order flow so persistent?", *JEDC* 2015; arXiv 1108.1632), LSE data with member IDs: "On timescales of less than a few hours the persistence of order flow is overwhelmingly due to splitting rather than herding"; at longer horizons different investors' behaviour is anti-correlated, due to differential response to price-changing vs non-price-changing market orders — [arXiv 1108.1632](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.1632v1); [CFM](https://www.cfm.com/?p=1638)
- Benzaquen & Bouchaud: a continuous power-law distribution of memory times lets the temporal decay of impact reconcile persistent order flow with diffusive prices ("multiscale latent order book... escape the diffusivity paradox") — [UvA winter school slides](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/a.khedher/winterschool/18BenzaquenBouchaud.pdf); [arXiv 1710.03734](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.03734)

**Concave (square-root) impact and its universality**
- Kyle's classical model predicts linear impact, so "the square root is not accounted for in Kyle's classical model"; sub-linear impact "implies that liquidity responds dynamically and adapts to soften the impact of larger orders" — [Scientia profile of Bouchaud](https://scientia.global/wp-content/uploads/Prof-Jean-Philippe-Bouchaud.pdf)
- Toth et al. (*Phys. Rev. X* 2011, arXiv 1105.1694): latent order book with V-shaped average supply/demand profile vanishing at the current price; "the square-root law is the result of the anomalously small local liquidity that induces a breakdown of linear response"; "large metaorders have to be fragmented in order to be digested by the liquidity funnel, which leads to a long memory in the sign of the order flow" — [arXiv 1105.1694](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694v3); [Institut Louis Bachelier](https://institutlouisbachelier.org/anomalous-price-impact-and-the-critical-nature-of-liquidity-in-financial-markets)
- Donier, Bonart, Mastromatteo, Bouchaud (*QF* 2015, arXiv 1412.0141): locally linear latent book evolving as reaction-diffusion with one emergent parameter (liquidity L) "accounts for the universally observed square-root impact law," predicts non-trivial trajectories when trading is interrupted/reversed, is "free of price manipulation," and splits price into a transient "mechanical" and a permanent "informational" component — [arXiv 1412.0141](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0141v3); [EconPapers QF](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:15:y:2015:i:7:p:1109-1121)
- Bouchaud (2018 talk): the strict square-root form holds in the large participation-ratio limit; most data sit at Q/V ~ 0.1-10% — [Imperial FIPS2018 slides](https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~ajacquie/FIPS2018/Slides/LondonFIPS2018%20-%20Bouchaud.pdf)
- **Alternative route:** Kyle & Obizhaeva market microstructure invariance (*Econometrica* 2016): "the dollar size of bets increases at a rate proportional to the square root of the number of bets per day"; invariance "can be consistent with either linear or square-root models"; empirically a square-root model fits 90th-99th percentile orders better, a linear model fits the largest 1% slightly better; "Except for the knife-edged case of the square root market impact function, additional assumptions... are needed to apply the same market impact formula to all assets" — [Kyle-Obizhaeva ECTA 2016 PDF](https://pages.nes.ru/aobizhaeva/Kyle-Obizhaeva-ECTA-2016-Invariance-with-Supplement.pdf); [NES WP "The Market Impact Puzzle"](https://www.nes.ru/files/Preprints-resh/WP232.pdf); Fed test on US stock trades — [FEDS 2016](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/microstructure-invariance-in-us-stock-market-trades.htm)
- Crash test: 1987 crash and 2008 Société Générale liquidation fell "by magnitudes similar to predictions from invariance" — [NBER conf. 2009 Obizhaeva](https://projects.nber.org/confer/2009/MMf09/obizhaeva.pdf)
- **Against strong universality:** reanalysis of the 3/2 law: "the 3/2 law is very robust, the invariant I is actually quite far from invariant, as it varies from one asset to the other and across time" — weak universality favoured — [arXiv 1902.03457](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03457)
- Farmer-Lillo (KQ1): LSE/NYSE average impact "grows much slower than a square root law" when long-memory order flow is properly handled — [arXiv cond-mat/0309416](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0309416)

**Latent vs displayed liquidity**
- The latent order book "holds the intentions of low-frequency traders, and some of these may never show up in the visible book" — [Donier et al. summary via UvA slides](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/a.khedher/winterschool/18DonierEtAl.pdf); Lemhadri extends with mean-reverting agents and calls Donier et al. "one of the most promising agent-based models for market impact" — [arXiv 1802.06101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06101v4.pdf)
- "The long-term fate of uninformed trades and their price impact remains an open question" — [Scientia profile](https://www.scientia.global/professor-jean-philippe-bouchaud-the-inelastic-market-hypothesis-explaining-the-origins-of-financial-fluctuations/)
- No source retrieved quantifies the displayed-depth-to-traded-volume ratio — see Gaps.

**Sudden liquidity evaporation / flash crashes**
- Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi, Tuzun (*J. Finance* 2017): May 6 2010 — "a large automated selling program was rapidly executed in the E-mini S&P 500"; HFT "trading pattern... did not change when prices fell"; "High Frequency Traders did not trigger the Flash Crash, but their responses to the unusually large selling pressure on that day exacerbated market volatility"; >15,000 accounts classified into six categories; the CFTC-SEC report's narrative "was based in part on the preliminary analysis contained in the original version of this paper" — [CFTC OCE PDF](https://www.cftc.gov/idc/groups/public/@economicanalysis/documents/file/oce_flashcrash0314.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/ab_id=1686004); [JF DOI](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jofi.12498)
- Easley, Lopez de Prado, O'Hara: "the 'flash crash' is the result of the new dynamics at play in the current market structure"; it "might have been avoided had liquidity providers remained in the marketplace"; propose VPIN flow-toxicity metric — [SSRN 1695041](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1695041)
- CEPR/VoxEU: a large share of liquidity now comes from providers (HFTs, hedge funds) with no formal quoting obligation who "choose whether to supply liquidity based on the profit opportunities they perceive" — [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/index%2ephp/voxeu/columns/why-liquidity-evaporates-when-it-most-needed)
- Bouchaud (2025 essay) links liquidity collapse to Glosten-Milgrom adverse selection: when trades are judged too informative no spread lets providers break even, so liquidity disappears — an amplification loop — [Bouchaud Substack: Endogenous liquidity crises](https://bouchaud.substack.com/p/endogenous-liquidity-crises) (summary only; page could not be fetched)
- A Sarao-related study: "market conditions alone could produce a flash crash without fundamental information" — [SSRN 2721922 mirror](https://www.findknowdo.com/sites/default/files/news/attachments/2016/01/ssrn-id2721922.pdf); Brewer, Cvitanic, Plott reproduced a flash crash in a lab/simulation by submitting an extremely large order and tracking depth drain — [UCEMA PDF](https://ucema.edu.ar/publicaciones/download/volume16/brewer.pdf); Kyle-Obizhaeva "Large bets and stock market crashes" — [RevFin summary](https://revfin.org/?p=3154); state-of-the-art review — [Emerald SEF](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/SEF-07-2018-0223/full/html)

**Endogeneity of jumps**
- See KQ2: Joulin et al. 2008 (jumps "induced by order flow fluctuations close to the point of vanishing liquidity"; volume not responsible); Bouchaud group ~95% of idiosyncratic jumps without news — [arXiv 2106.07040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07040v1); Ait-Sahalia & Li 2024 — [NBER w32746](https://nber.org/papers/w32746); CFM 2010 "endogenous dynamics of markets: price impact and feedback loops" — [CFM PDF](https://www.cfm.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/269-2010-the-endogenous-dynamics-of-markets-price-impact-and-feedback-loops.pdf)

**Intraday seasonality (U-shape)**
- Admati & Pfleiderer (*RFS* 1988) is "a partial explanation": "liquidity traders and informed traders are motivated to concentrate their trades in certain periods because execution costs are low in those periods"; U-shaped volume can arise from demand shocks accumulated during market closure — [SUFE summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/355980); [EconWPA 9507008](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/fin/papers/9507/9507008.pdf)
- Helsinki evidence: informed and liquidity traders concentrate at open and close, "indicating strategic behavior" — [EFMA 2005 Comerton-Forde](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2005-Milan/papers/55-comerton-forde_paper.pdf); Treasuries: volatility and volume persist for over an hour after announcements — [FRBNY research paper 9633](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/historical/frbny/researchpapers/frbny_researchpapers_9633.pdf)
- **Tension:** U-shaped volume/volatility on NYSE "would imply an inverted U-shape for execution costs, but bid-ask spreads have a U-shaped pattern, and after accounting for trading within the spread, execution costs rise steadily over the day"; "neither the Admati-Pfleiderer model nor our model can generate either a U-shaped pattern or an upward sloping pattern for those execution costs" — [Toronto tecipa-365](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-365.pdf)

### Inferences
- Marker: **long-memory order flow + martingale prices** — SETTLED-REGULARITY; the order-splitting origin of the persistence is PARTIAL-CONSENSUS (Toth et al. with member IDs is strong evidence); the *mechanism of compensation* (asymmetric liquidity, propagator decay, multi-timescale latent book) is OPEN. **Square-root impact** — regularity broadly accepted across asset classes; exact exponent/universality CONTESTED (Farmer-Lillo "slower than square root"; Kyle-Obizhaeva "linear fits the largest 1%"; 3/2-law reanalysis "weak universality"); mechanism OPEN between latent-liquidity criticality and invariance/bet-size scaling. **Latent liquidity** — a modelling construct with indirect support; no direct measurement retrieved. **Flash crashes** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS that they are endogenous amplification (selling program + withdrawal of unobligated liquidity providers), not news; the role of HFT is "exacerbated, did not trigger." **Endogenous jumps** — SETTLED-REGULARITY / OPEN-MECHANISM. **Intraday U-shape** — regularity settled; the Admati-Pfleiderer strategic-concentration model is only partial and contradicted by execution-cost patterns.
- Discriminating observations: (1) latent-liquidity vs invariance: the Donier et al. model predicts specific impact trajectories when a metaorder is *interrupted or reversed* and a mechanical/informational decomposition of post-trade reversion; invariance predicts bet-size scaling with the square root of bets/day across assets. Both can be checked on the same metaorder database. (2) Square-root exponent: estimate impact conditioning on participation rate Q/V and on order-flow autocorrelation (Farmer-Lillo correction) — if the exponent is ~0.5 only in the high-participation limit, the econophysics reading holds. (3) Flash-crash endogeneity: in the absence of news, the ratio of quote cancellations to trades and the displayed depth at best should fall *before* the price acceleration if withdrawal drives it, and after if the selling program drives it (Kirilenko et al.'s account-level data design is the template). (4) Jumps: match jump times to a news feed at second resolution and compare post-jump volatility relaxation; a news jump and a liquidity jump should relax differently (Joulin et al.).

### Gaps
- No retrieved source quantifies displayed depth as a fraction of daily traded volume ("latent liquidity problem" numbers); the latent-book literature argues it indirectly.
- Full text of Toth et al. 2011/Donier et al. 2015 not accessible; the specific trajectory predictions on interrupted metaorders are described only qualitatively here.
- The CFTC-SEC joint report (Sept 30 2010) itself was not retrieved; its conclusions are described secondhand via Kirilenko et al.
- Trade-sign/price-response dependence structure: only the Bouchaud-Gefen-Potters-Wyart propagator paper was retrieved; the Lillo-Farmer 2004 long-memory paper and the Wyart et al. spread/volatility relation were not surfaced.
- No commodity-futures-specific square-root-impact or latent-liquidity study was found; all cited impact work is equities.

## KQ4. Momentum, reversal and the momentum crash

### Takeaway
Trend-following/time-series momentum is profitable over more than a century across asset classes, and short-term reversal coexists with it as the return to liquidity provision that spikes under stress (Nagel); the *cause* of momentum is still split between behavioral under/over-reaction, risk/hedging premia and (more recently) flows, and the search could not retrieve a formal review settling this. Momentum crashes (Daniel-Moskowitz) are well characterized and partly forecastable, which is itself a puzzle for a pure risk story.

### Cited Findings
- Moskowitz, Ooi, Pedersen (2012): "Time series momentum has been profitable on average since 1985 for nearly all equity index futures, fixed income futures, commodity futures and currency forwards" — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/time-series-momentum-effect/); the signal is an asset's own past return rather than its rank against peers — [Menon Lab explainer](https://themenonlab.blog/blog/time-series-momentum-hedge-fund-strategy-explained)
- Hurst, Ooi, Pedersen (AQR, "A Century of Evidence on Trend Following"): extended the test back to 1880 and found the strategy "consistently profitable over the next 110 years"; persistence attributed to "behavioral biases and non-profit seeking market participants, which... have likely existed for a long time"; traces trend-following to Ricardo's "cut short your losses" — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing); [PDF mirror](https://oxfordstrat.com/coasdfASD32/uploads/2016/03/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf)
- Explanations offered are "behavioral: markets underreact to new information, then overreact" and "risk-based, such as hedgers' premiums"; whether broad adoption has arbitraged away some of the edge "is debated" — [Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen PDF](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf); near-100-year equity TSM evidence — [Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/?p=1210204); equity and commodity TSM across markets — [Manchester PDF](https://pure.manchester.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/39718724/The_Trend_is_Your_Friend_Time_Series_Momentum_Strategies_Across_Equity_and_Commodity_Markets.pdf)
- Daniel & Moskowitz ("Momentum Crashes", *JFE* 2016; NBER WP 20439): "Despite their strong positive average returns across numerous asset classes, momentum strategies can experience infrequent and persistent strings of negative returns"; crashes occur after market declines when volatility is high; "These momentum crashes are partly forecastable"; past-loser decile beta "can rise above 3" after major declines; a dynamic strategy on forecast mean/variance "approximately doubles the alpha and Sharpe Ratio... and is not explained by other factors"; results hold in four equity markets plus bonds, commodities, currencies — [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439); [Columbia PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/4607/mom11.pdf); [ETF.com summary](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-downside-momentum); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/risk-of-momentum-crashes/)
- Byun & Jeon (2023): "Stocks far from peaks outperforming stocks near peaks during the market rebounds are the key to understanding the economic channel behind momentum crashes" — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=86891)
- One source claims time-series momentum in individual stocks "does not suffer from January losses and market crashes" — a claim about TSM in stocks, not the cross-sectional crash Daniel-Moskowitz study — [Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/?p=1210204)
- Nagel ("Evaporating Liquidity", *RFS* 2012, 25(7):2005-2039; NBER w17653): short-term reversal returns proxy returns to liquidity provision; expected returns "strongly time-varying and closely tied to the VIX index"; returns and Sharpe ratios climb sharply in 2007-09; attributed "mainly to liquidity suppliers pulling back," tied to financially constrained intermediaries (Adrian-Shin) — [NBER w17653](https://nber.org/papers/w17653); [EconPapers RFS](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:25:y:2012:i:7:p:2005-2039); [earlier draft](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Nagel_LiqSupply_9a.pdf)
- Related: Osler's FX evidence that stop-loss clustering beyond round numbers produces price cascades (see KQ8) is a mechanical, flow-based source of short-horizon trend continuation — [NY Fed SR 150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)

### Inferences
- Marker: **existence of TSM/trend** — SETTLED-REGULARITY across a century; **cause** — OPEN (behavioral vs risk/hedging-premium vs flow/mechanical). **Short-term reversal** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS that it is compensation for liquidity provision (Nagel), which neatly explains why it coexists with momentum: they operate at different horizons and on different agents (liquidity takers' slow metaorders vs liquidity providers' inventory). **Momentum crashes** — SETTLED-REGULARITY; the forecastability and the option-like payoff of the loser leg in rebounds are hard to reconcile with a pure risk-premium explanation, but a convincing behavioral or flow mechanism has not been nailed down in the retrieved sources.
- Discriminating observations: (1) risk vs behavior — if momentum is compensation for risk, its premium should rise when the covariance with the pricing kernel rises; Daniel-Moskowitz's finding that crashes are *forecastable* and *avoidable* by volatility scaling argues against a simple risk story. (2) flow vs behavior — if trend is driven by slow institutional metaorders (Bouchaud's long-memory order flow), trend returns should scale with the persistence of signed order flow at the asset level, which is measurable; a pure belief-based under-reaction story has no such order-flow signature. (3) For commodities specifically, the hedging-pressure explanation predicts trend profits concentrated where hedgers' net positions are one-sided (CFTC COT data) — testable per market.

### Gaps
- The search budget ran out before a formal review of momentum explanations (Jegadeesh-Titman 2011 or similar) could be retrieved; the behavioral/risk split above is sourced only from AQR practitioner papers.
- No source was found on commodity-specific trend-following explanations (hedging pressure vs. storage/backwardation roll).
- No source on why reversal and momentum have the specific horizon boundary they do (approx. 1 month vs 12 months) beyond the liquidity-provision interpretation.

## KQ5. Flow-based pricing: inelastic markets and the commodity financialization debate

### Takeaway
The inelastic markets hypothesis (price multiplier ~5x per dollar of flow) is an actively contested hypothesis whose identification is under direct attack (van Binsbergen-David-Opp: elasticity estimates "off by orders of magnitude"), while in commodities the Masters hypothesis (index flows caused the 2007-08 boom) is rejected by most studies (Irwin-Sanders, Hamilton-Wu) but Singleton (2014) and Tang-Xiong (2012) find significant flow effects and increased index-commodity co-movement — the resolution most authors land on is "financialization changed co-movement and risk-sharing without creating a bubble."

### Cited Findings

**Inelastic markets**
- Gabaix & Koijen: aggregate demand elasticity is small because "households place money with institutions that face constraints, such as mandates to keep a fixed equity share"; GIV estimate "$1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5"; buybacks and government equity purchases have sizable effects; "the 'inelastic market hypothesis' remains a hypothesis" — [NBER w28967](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28967); [NBER conference PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f141240.pdf); blog view that "the authors do not provide especially compelling evidence" — [Real Investment Advice](https://realinvestmentadvice.com/?p=457709)
- **Critiques:** van Binsbergen, David, Opp (NBER WP 34528): "if price shifts exhibit momentum or persistence, the resulting estimates can no longer be interpreted as price elasticities"; the literature's instruments "yield elasticity estimates that are off by orders of magnitude, or even have the wrong sign"; but "does this imply that asset markets are inelastic? Our answer is: not at all" — [NBER w34528 rev0](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34528/revisions/w34528.rev0.pdf); [Bocconi PDF](https://finance.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/Paper%20Jules%20van%20Binsbergen.pdf)
- Discussant slides list two critique lines: "markets are not inelastic" (Ross 1987 on close substitutes) and "improper to apply a demand system to stocks" (cross-asset complementarities, Fuchs-Fukuda-Neuhann; dynamic persistence, van Binsbergen et al.) — [MFA 2026 discussion](https://sangmino.github.io/Documents/20260312_MFA2026_Oh_Discussion.pdf)
- van der Beck, Bretscher, Fu "A Bound on Price Impact and Disagreement": model-free bound from log-linear demand and market clearing; bounds "align closely with reduced-form estimates from quasi-experiments, such as price impacts from index reconstitutions" — [priceimpactbound.github.io](https://priceimpactbound.github.io/PriceImpactBound/main.pdf); related "Rethinking Volume" — [rethinkingvolume.github.io](https://rethinkingvolume.github.io/RethinkingVolume/main.pdf)
- Kargar, Li et al.: low elasticity estimates "are not puzzling," attributable to unspanned returns and near-isoelastic demand — [SSE ffn_demand PDF](https://www.hhs.se/contentassets/69d7d96d66144cc69ec0cb4ae90d9035/ffn_demand.pdf)
- Bouchaud's Latent Liquidity Model is presented as the microstructural bridge from the intraday square-root law to Gabaix-Koijen's macro multiplier — [Scientia](https://www.scientia.global/professor-jean-philippe-bouchaud-the-inelastic-market-hypothesis-explaining-the-origins-of-financial-fluctuations/)

**Commodity index investment (Masters hypothesis / financialization)**
- Definition: "The Masters Hypothesis is the assertion that large investment inflows into long-only commodity index funds pushed prices far above fundamental value" — [Sanders & Irwin NCCC-134 2015](https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/meetings/nccc134/conf_2015/pdf/Sanders_Irwin_NCCC_134_2015.pdf)
- Irwin & Sanders: "Price behavior consistent with the Masters Hypothesis is surprisingly difficult to find in the data"; "Support for financialization impacts does not imply support for the Masters Hypothesis" — same source; Aulerich, Irwin, Garcia (NBER chapter): "financial index investment flows may cause market prices to deviate from fundamental values under certain theoretical conditions" but "Most previous studies reject the basic tenet of the Masters Hypothesis" for agriculture — [NBER c12814](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c12814.pdf)
- Hamilton & Wu, "Effects of index-fund investing on commodity futures prices" — [UCSD PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jhamilto/commodity_index.pdf) (could not be fetched; listed as a primary source on the sceptical side)
- Tang & Xiong (2012): co-movement trends "are stronger for those commodities that are part of a major index than for other commodities"; Irwin-Sanders read this as "a rational price effect related to increased market integration" — [Xiong Bank of Canada slides](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/wei-xiong.pdf); [Sanders-Irwin 2015](https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/assets/meetings/nccc134/conf_2015/pdf/Sanders_Irwin_NCCC_134_2015.pdf)
- Cheng & Xiong (2014): financialization "introduced additional risks" and "may have also led to information friction in commodity markets" — [Cambridge Core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DC24331A5E872817A0C62D696692E94B/core-reader)
- Singleton (*Management Science* 2014, 60(2):300-318): "economically and statistically significant effects of investor flows on futures prices, after controlling for stock-market returns, balance-sheet flexibility of large financial institutions, open interest, the futures/spot basis, and lagged oil futures returns"; largest impacts from intermediate-term growth in index positions and managed-money spread positions; "informational frictions and the associated speculative activity may induce prices to drift away from fundamental values" — [EconPapers MS](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:2:p:300-318); [EIA-hosted paper](https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/Paper-Boom%20and%20Bust%20in%20Oil%20Prices-Singleton.pdf); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/news/research/singleton_oil_2011.html)
- Discussant (Buyuksahin): Singleton "uses imputed CIT position based on Masters (2008) methodology" — [EIA discussant comments](https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/Comments%20by%20Buyuksahin%20on%20Boom%20and%20Bust%20Paper.pdf); a 2008 CFTC task force concluded "changes in fundamental factors provide the best explanation for the recent crude oil price increases" — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/insights/investor-flows-were-behind-2008-oil-price-wild-ride)
- Better Markets (advocacy) critique of Irwin-Sanders' OECD reports: the index-flow/price link "is not limited to oil" (citing Tang-Xiong 2009) — [IATP-hosted PDF](https://iatp.org/sites/default/files/451_2_107621.pdf); [Better Markets](https://bettermarkets.org/newsroom/challenge-conventional-wisdom-speculators-0)

### Inferences
- Marker: **inelastic markets** — OPEN/CONTESTED, with the live dispute about identification (persistence contaminates elasticity estimates) rather than about whether flows matter at all. **Masters hypothesis (bubble)** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS against. **Financialization changed co-movement/risk-sharing** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS for. **Singleton's flow effects in oil** — a minority but serious finding whose weakness (imputed index positions) is acknowledged by its discussant.
- For commodities, the two literatures have never been joined in the retrieved sources: nobody has computed a Gabaix-Koijen-style dollar multiplier for a futures market, where (unlike equities) net supply is zero and every long is matched by a short, so the "inelastic" argument must run through hedger/arbitrageur capital constraints rather than fixed-mandate households.
- Discriminating observations: (1) exogenous flow shocks with no information content — index roll dates, Goldman roll, ETF creation/redemption, Chinese retail product unwinds (cf. Yuan You Bao, KQ6) — give a clean multiplier and a reversal profile: permanent (inelastic) vs transitory (liquidity); (2) van Binsbergen et al.'s critique is testable by checking whether estimated elasticities fall as the measurement horizon lengthens past the persistence horizon of the instrument; (3) for financialization, the Tang-Xiong index/non-index co-movement gap should have narrowed after 2012-14 when index AUM fell — a natural out-of-sample test.

### Gaps
- Hamilton & Wu's paper (method/findings) could not be fetched; its conclusion is known only as "sceptical side."
- No source was retrieved applying the inelastic-markets framework to commodity futures or estimating a flow multiplier for NG/CL.
- The full text of Tang-Xiong (2012) and Cheng-Xiong (2014) were not retrieved; summaries are from slides and secondary citations.
- No post-2015 update of Irwin-Sanders on the financialization debate was surfaced.

## KQ6. Commodity-specific puzzles (natural gas volatility, futures-as-forecasts, storage-surprise asymmetry, negative WTI, Samuelson effect, widowmaker, seasonality, weather, spreads)

### Takeaway
Most commodity-specific regularities are documented but under-mechanised: the Samuelson effect is *absent* in most contracts and strongest in ag/energy; the EIA storage report is a documented volatility event with an unexplained asymmetric *pre-announcement* return; the April 2020 negative WTI print was officially attributed to "fundamental and technical factors" with the regulator declining to identify a root cause and academics pointing at TAS-related closing pressure; futures are roughly unbiased for energy but generally no better than a random walk; the Amaranth widowmaker loss is well documented but a study found no statistically significant effect of its positions on prices or spreads.

### Cited Findings

**Why natural gas is so volatile**
- Pindyck (CEEPR 2003, "Volatility in Natural Gas and Oil Markets"): daily futures data since 1990; "crude oil returns are a significant predictor of natural gas returns (but not the other way around), and crude oil volatility is a significant predictor of natural gas volatility"; "Shocks to volatility are generally short-lived"; persistent volatility changes alter incentives to invest in inventories and transport — [CEEPR](https://ceepr.mit.edu/workingpaper/volatility-in-natural-gas-and-oil-markets-2/); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/45005)
- Pindyck (2001 primer): oil products, natural gas and many commodities "are characterized by high levels of volatility"; links volatility to inventories and the marginal value of storage — [primer PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-dynamics-of-commodity-spot-and-futures-markets-a-primer-4id8u0qnbn.pdf)
- Mu (2007, *Energy Economics* 29(1):46-63): "significant weather effect on both the conditional mean and the conditional volatility of natural gas futures returns"; volatility higher on Mondays and storage-report days, read as "information about market fundamentals is an important determinant"; "Weather affects about fifty percent of the U.S. natural gas demand" — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v29y2007i1p46-63.html); [2004 OU working paper](https://iaee.org/en/students/best_papers/Xiaoyi_Mu2.pdf)
- Winter-contract volatility "is naturally high... since little inventory typically carries over from winter to spring in a normal year" — [CORE 195644551](https://core.ac.uk/download/195644551.pdf)
- No source retrieved ranks NG as "the most volatile commodity" or decomposes its volatility into storage-constraint, weather and pipeline-constraint components — see Gaps.

**Futures as forecasts of spot (the Meese-Rogoff analogue)**
- Chinn & Coibion (NBER w15830): "energy futures prices are generally unbiased predictors of future spot prices, but there is much stronger evidence against the null for other commodity markets"; difference "driven in part by the depth of each market"; futures "do approximately as well as a random walk... and vastly outperform a reduced form empirical model" — [NBER w15830](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15830/w15830.pdf)
- Reeve & Vigfusson (Fed IFDP 1025): futures "generally outperformed a random walk forecast, but not by a large margin"; "outperform a random walk by a considerable margin when there is a sizeable difference between spot and futures prices" — [EconPapers IFDP 1025](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedgif:1025); Fed Note (Sept 2026): futures "do not in general produce unbiased forecasts," but when futures deviate >5% from spot they beat the random walk for most maturities — [FEDS Notes 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/revisiting-the-forecasting-potential-of-futures-prices-20260903.html)
- Natural gas futures are a biased predictor at 3- and 6-month horizons — [NBER w11033](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11033/w11033.pdf)
- Alquist & Kilian (*JAE* 2010, 25(4):539-573): "no theoretical support for the common view that oil futures prices are good predictors of the spot price in the mean-squared error sense"; "futures-based forecasts are biased and typically inferior to... the no-change forecast"; the negative basis "may be viewed as an index of fluctuations in the price of crude oil driven by precautionary demand" — [CEPR DP6548 summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/DP6548.html); [CREI PDF](https://crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/killian.pdf); Alquist-Kilian-Vigfusson: "none of these alternative forecasting approaches outperforms the no-change forecast" — [Fed IFDP](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/forecasting-the-price-of-oil.htm); replication (Anton 2017/18): results hold but "relative accuracy of the random walk forecast and the futures-based forecast changes when the sample is extended to August 2016" — [EconPapers JAE 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:33:y:2018:i:1:p:160-163)
- Moosa (post-Keynesian critique): futures "produce small forecasting errors, because they are related contemporaneously to spot prices," but "fail to capture turning points and exhibit signs of biasedness and inefficiency" — [EconPapers PKE 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:mes:postke:v:43:y:2020:i:3:p:391-416)
- Outperformance vs random walk "is seldom statistically significant" — [Econbrowser 2009](https://econbrowser.com/archives/2009/10/futures_as_pred)
- Bank of Canada paper rejects pure random walk for nominal and real oil prices — [Central Banking](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banking/research/2075987/oil-prices-follow-random-walk-bank-canada-paper)

**Storage-report reactions and asymmetry**
- Linn & Zhu (*JFM* 2004, 24(3):283-313): "the weekly gas storage report announcement was responsible for considerable volatility at the time of its release, and... volatility up to 30 minutes following the announcement was also higher than normal"; covers AGA era and EIA takeover after May 6 2002 — [OU PDF](https://ou.edu/content/dam/price/Management/Energy%20Institute/docs/Linn%20and%20Zhu%20JFM%202004.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v24y2004i3p283-313.html); effect persisted under EIA — [Reading eprint](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/90003/1/NG_paper_final_round.pdf)
- Prokopczuk, Wese Simen, Wichmann, "The Natural Gas Announcement Day Puzzle" (*Energy Journal* 2021): "more than 50% of the annual return is earned on these days"; "the pre-announcement return is entirely realized on days where the announcement surprise is positive," which "casts doubt on a simple explanation based on informed trading" — [IAEE exec summary](https://iaee.org/ej/ejexec/ej42-2-Wichmann-exsum.pdf); [IDEAS EJ 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/enejou/v40y2019i5p121-142.html)

**Samuelson effect and exceptions**
- Daal & Farhat: 6,805 contracts across 61 commodities, "the maturity effect is absent in the majority of contracts"; with Wei (2006, *Rev. Fin. ### Inferences
- Markers: **NG volatility level** — regularity accepted; decomposition OPEN (storage/weather/pipeline each have evidence, no joint attribution retrieved). **Futures-as-forecasts** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS: unbiased-ish for deep energy markets, biased elsewhere, rarely significantly better than no-change; the useful conditional (large basis -> futures beat RW) is replicated by Reeve-Vigfusson and the 2026 Fed note. **Storage-report asymmetry** — the *post*-announcement asymmetry is practitioner lore with no retrieved systematic test; the *pre*-announcement asymmetry (returns only ahead of positive surprises) is a documented OPEN puzzle (Prokopczuk et al.) that is inconsistent with simple informed trading. **Negative WTI** — official explanation PARTIAL (fundamentals + unspecified "technical factors"); academic work converges on expiry/TAS closing pressure as the technical factor; no root-cause finding exists. **Samuelson effect** — CONTESTED-FACT as a general law; PARTIAL-CONSENSUS it holds in ag/energy and is explained there by the Bessembinder covariance mechanism. **Widowmaker** — the loss is documented; whether Amaranth's size moved the spread is contested (Bentek/Till yes, the statistical study no). **Seasonality** — stochastic and non-stationary; a fixed calendar anomaly is not supported.
- Reading the 2020 episode and the Amaranth episode together: both are *expiry/term-structure mechanics* events where a concentrated position met a delivery or roll constraint; the market's "fundamentals" (Cushing storage, winter scarcity) set the stage, but the magnitude came from positioning and contract design. That is the practitioner view ("the WHEN is positioning, the WHY is fundamentals") and it is consistent with — not proven by — the retrieved sources.
- Discriminating observations: (1) storage asymmetry — a per-event study pairing surprise sign and magnitude with pre-announcement positioning (COT managed money) and the price response at 1-sec/1-min resolution; if response asymmetry is explained by positioning, the "asymmetry" is a crowding effect, not a fundamental one. (2) Negative WTI — the TAS-closing-pressure story predicts that TAS volume and the TAS-vs-outright basis in the final hour were anomalous relative to prior expiries; the storage story predicts that Cushing-delivery bids and physical WTI (Midland/Houston) traded commensurately negative — Berkovitz's complaint is precisely that the physical-futures disconnect was not analyzed. (3) Samuelson — test the Bessembinder covariance prediction directly: contracts where spot and carry costs covary negatively should show the effect; those where they do not should not. (4) Weather — regress price response on forecast *revision* size at each horizon and on forecast skill (ensemble spread); the Roll/Boudoukh lesson is to condition on nonlinear demand thresholds (HDD/CDD regime).

### Gaps

- The CFTC Interim Report PDF, Berkovitz statement and Columbia widowmaker piece could not be fetched (network allowlist); specifics (e.g., TAS volume numbers, concentration figures) are not in these notes.
- Chincarini's own 2006/2007 papers were not retrieved.
- No study of price reaction to weather-model forecast revisions (GFS/ECMWF run changes) vs realized weather was found.
- No commodity-specific lead-lag test of calendar spreads vs outrights was found; the farmdoc paper addresses information content, not precedence.
- Energy equivalents of Fair (2002) — large CL/NG moves matched to news at minute resolution — were not retrieved.

## KQ7. Forecasting puzzles

### Takeaway
The commodity analogue of Meese-Rogoff holds: for oil, nothing in the standard toolkit reliably beats the no-change forecast out of sample (Alquist-Kilian-Vigfusson), agency forecasts (EIA) are *worse* than no-change, and model combinations gain only ~12-18% in MSE; the one robust conditional improvement is that futures beat the random walk when the basis is large. At intraday horizons the only robustly documented predictive content retrieved is short-horizon order-flow/imbalance effects (KQ3/KQ8), not price-level forecasts.

### Cited Findings
- Meese-Rogoff analogy drawn explicitly: as with exchange-rate forwards (Meese & Rogoff 1983; Engel 1996; Cheung-Chinn-Garcia Pascual 2005), "metals futures do not typically outperform random walks in terms of squared forecast errors"; energy and ag futures show stronger predictive content; oil forecasts "could have improved by using other energy futures" — [Aberdeen DP 16-8](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/files/69167574/DP_16_8.pdf)
- Alquist, Kilian, Vigfusson: "none of these alternative forecasting approaches outperforms the no-change forecast of the nominal price of oil" — [Fed IFDP "Forecasting the price of oil"](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/forecasting-the-price-of-oil.htm); Baumeister & Kilian (*IER* 2014): monthly-data VARs give the most accurate real-time quarterly forecasts among a wide range of methods — [EconPapers IER](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:iecrev:v:55:y:2014:i:3:p:869-889)
- Baumeister & Kilian forecast combinations: MSE reductions "up to 18% relative to the best single model, and directional accuracy as high as 77%" (2013 DP) / "up to 12% and 72%" (JBES 2015); "EIA oil price forecasts tend to be less accurate than no-change forecasts and... adding them to the combination lowers its accuracy" — [CEPR DP9569](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP9569.pdf); [EconPapers JBES 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:33:y:2015:i:3:p:338-351)
- Kunze, Spiwoks, Bizer, Windels (*MDE* 2018) on survey forecasts: forecasters tend to beat naive predictions at longer horizons though trend adjustments become more pronounced there (abstract only) — [Zendy MDE](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/mde.2916)
- ECB WP 1735 (Brent): "considerable instability in the performance of all models"; combining improves accuracy especially at longer horizons — [ECB WP 1735](https://ECB.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecbwp1735.pdf)
- Replication of Alquist-Kilian to 2016: relative accuracy of RW vs futures "changes when the sample is extended" — [EconPapers JAE 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:33:y:2018:i:1:p:160-163); conditional improvement when |basis|>5% — [FEDS Notes 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/revisiting-the-forecasting-potential-of-futures-prices-20260903.html)
- Intraday predictive content: Evans-Lyons' order-flow R^2 > 60% on DEM/USD daily returns is the benchmark for flow-based explanatory (not necessarily forecasting) power — [NBER w9893](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9893.pdf); GJR claim improved realized-volatility forecasts from the RFSV model — [arXiv 1410.3394](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3394); Daniel-Moskowitz: momentum crashes "partly forecastable" from volatility state — [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439); Nagel: reversal returns forecastable from VIX — [NBER w17653](https://nber.org/papers/w17653)

### Inferences
- Marker: **random walk hard to beat for commodity price levels** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS (strong for oil; mixed for other commodities). The documented exceptions are all *conditional* (large basis; stressed VIX state; post-crash momentum beta) and are about second moments or regime-dependent risk premia rather than unconditional level forecasts.
- Why professionals are poor: the retrieved evidence points to (i) anchoring to the futures curve, which is itself near-RW; (ii) trend-extrapolation at long horizons (Kunze et al.); (iii) model instability (ECB). None of these sources tests the "forecasters are paid to be confident, not accurate" incentive story — unsourced here.
- The contrast between near-zero level predictability and strong *explanatory* power of contemporaneous order flow (Evans-Lyons) is the macro form of the KQ3 efficiency paradox: flows explain what happened, not what will happen, except over the microstructure horizon where flow persistence (order splitting) gives mechanical short-term predictability.
- Discriminating observation: a forecasting horse race that includes (a) no-change, (b) futures, (c) basis-conditional futures, (d) order-flow/positioning state variables, evaluated per regime (backwardation vs contango; high vs low VIX/OVX) rather than pooled — the pooled MSE comparisons in the literature may be hiding regime-local predictability.

### Gaps
- No source on Consensus Economics / Bloomberg survey accuracy for oil or NG specifically.
- No retrieved study of intraday (minute-to-hour) price-level predictability in energy futures.
- Full text of Baumeister-Kilian papers not accessed; figures are from abstracts.

## KQ8. Practitioner puzzles: "why did the market do that", absorption/exhaustion, stop runs, is order flow informative or hedging?

### Takeaway
Practitioner concepts (absorption, exhaustion, stop runs) have partial academic counterparts: Osler documents stop-loss/take-profit clustering around round numbers and stop-loss cascades in FX, and the "informative vs hedging" debate over order flow has a mixed empirical record that depends on customer type and horizon; the absorption/exhaustion dichotomy itself has no retrieved academic test and its vendor literature concedes it is "a read, not a guarantee."

### Cited Findings

**Stop runs / order clustering**
- Osler (*J. Finance* 2003, 58(5):1791-1819): "Take-profit orders cluster particularly strongly at round numbers... Stop-loss orders cluster strongly just beyond round numbers," offering an explanation for the predictive success of support/resistance technical analysis — [IDEAS JF](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v58y2003i5p1791-1819.html); [NY Fed SR 125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf)
- Osler (NY Fed SR 150): "executed stop-loss sell orders cluster just below round numbers; stop-loss buy orders cluster just above"; if stops trigger in waves "the response to stop-loss orders should be larger, and should last longer, than the response to take-profit orders" (stop-loss cascades) — [NY Fed SR 150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)
- Caveats: data from a single dealing bank's order book, 1996-98 major FX pairs; the papers show clustering and price response, "not... deliberate 'hunting' by anyone" — [EconPapers JF](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:58:y:2003:i:5:p:1791-1819); on technical analysis generally "there is no consensus on this conundrum in the academic community, as both opposing sides have equally numerous and convincing academic evidence" — [arXiv 2101.07410](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07410)

**Is order flow informative or hedging?**
- Evans & Lyons (2002, 2005, 2006) "argue that much of the impact of order flow in their data is permanent"; the 2002 R^2 > 60% on DEM/USD is the benchmark — [NBER w9893](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9893.pdf); [Georgetown announcements paper](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/evansm1/wpapers_files/Announcements.pdf)
- Fed IFDP 830 (same interdealer data) supports only a "weak flow-centric" view: "global interdealer order flow has an undeniable short- and medium-term" effect, not the strong permanent claim; Breedon & Vitale reach different conclusions on brokered data — [Fed IFDP 830](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2005/830/revision/ifdp830.htm)
- Customer flow: "Long spans of daily customer-to-dealer order flow, each from a single institution, were used by Evans and Lyons (2005) and Froot and Ramadorai (2005) to reach their opposite conclusions"; Cao, Evans, Lyons argue inventory information forecasts discount factors — a price effect not requiring superior cash-flow information — [NBER w8491](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8491.pdf); [CORE 296104228](https://core.ac.uk/download/296104228.pdf)
- Customer heterogeneity: corporate and private clients' trades "are generally uninformative and tend to lose money, while real-money investors' flows contain information about future returns"; hedge-fund flows forecast only transitory movements — [AEA 2013 paper](https://aeastaging.aeaweb.org/conference/2013/retrieve.php?pdfid=122); Rime, Sarno, Sojli on order flow and macro information — [Cass PDF](https://www.cass.city.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/78667/rime_sarno_sojli_n.pdf)
- Microstructure side: Bouchaud et al. estimate "the fraction of truly informed market orders is quite small" and per-trade information "on average very small" — [arXiv cond-mat/0307332](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0307332v2); Donier et al. split impact into transient mechanical and permanent informational components — [arXiv 1412.0141](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0141v3); "the long-term fate of uninformed trades and their price impact remains an open question" — [Scientia](https://www.scientia.global/professor-jean-philippe-bouchaud-the-inelastic-market-hypothesis-explaining-the-origins-of-financial-fluctuations/)

**Absorption vs exhaustion**
- Practitioner definitions: absorption = "passive liquidity responding to aggressive flow"; exhaustion = the state when that response persists / "dwindling volume on the final push" — [Beyond Candlesticks](https://beyondcandlesticks.substack.com/p/understanding-order-flow-pt-2); [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/absorption-and-exhaustion/); [Optimus Futures](https://optimusfutures.com/blog/footprint-chart-absorption-vs-exhaustion-explained-futurestrading/)
- Cautions from the same literature: "neither read guarantees a turn"; exhaustion "is harder to time because thin volume appears in quiet continuation"; "false reads multiply in thin or overnight sessions"; the same activity "can mean initiative, liquidation, hedging, or short covering"; "a read, not a guarantee" — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/absorption-and-exhaustion.md); [Tradezella](https://www.tradezella.com/learning-items/order-flow-terms-and-concepts); [Tickmill digest](https://recapio.com/digest/order-flow-events-absorption-exhaustion-momentum-by-tickmill); [Optimus](https://optimusfutures.com/blog/?p=19691)
- No backtest or peer-reviewed test of absorption/exhaustion signals was found in any retrieved source.

**"Why did the market do that" (large moves with no story)**
- The academic form of this complaint is the KQ2 no-news literature (Cutler-Poterba-Summers; Fair; Joulin et al.; Ait-Sahalia-Li), and in energy the practitioner record of storage-day reversals and "odd" reactions (KQ6, NGI items). The storage-day items record that identical surprise signs produced opposite moves within a week — [NGI](https://www.naturalgasintel.com/futures-plummet-on-large-storage-fill/); [NGI](https://www.naturalgasintel.com/?p=99524)

### Inferences
- Marker: **stop-loss clustering and cascades** — PARTIAL-CONSENSUS in FX (one bank's data); "stop hunting" as deliberate behaviour — OPEN/unproven. **Order flow informative vs hedging** — OPEN; the best-supported synthesis is "depends on who is trading and at what horizon": real-money/leveraged-informed flow has permanent impact, corporate/retail/hedging flow is transitory, and most individual trades carry almost no information. **Absorption/exhaustion** — practitioner lore, untested academically; its closest academic cousins are Toth et al.'s "turning point = far-side liquidity" latent-book dynamics and the Glosten-Milgrom withdrawal argument in Bouchaud's liquidity-crisis essay, but no retrieved paper operationalizes the dichotomy.
- The practitioner "it's just hedging flow" vs "that's informed selling" debate maps onto Donier et al.'s mechanical/informational decomposition: in principle the *decay profile* after the flow stops discriminates (mechanical impact reverts, informational does not). That is a testable per-event fingerprint, consistent with the house rule of per-event, never-pooled analysis.
- Discriminating observations: (1) stop runs — a market with visible stop/limit order books (or exchange-reported stop triggers) would let one test Osler's cascade asymmetry directly outside FX; (2) order-flow informativeness — classify flow by account type (Kirilenko-style audit trail) and measure permanent vs transitory impact per class; (3) absorption — define absorption as aggressive volume at a level with no price progress and test forward returns per cell (level type x session liquidity x flow imbalance), the LuxAlgo caution about thin sessions being the first control.

### Gaps
- No academic test of "absorption" or "exhaustion" as defined by footprint-chart practitioners was found.
- Stop-loss clustering evidence is FX-only and from the 1990s; nothing on futures/commodities was retrieved.
- Froot-Ramadorai (2005) and the Fed IFDP 830 full texts were not retrieved; their conclusions are summarized secondhand.
- No source retrieved on exchange-level evidence of deliberate stop hunting (enforcement actions, spoofing cases) — a plausible place to look is CFTC enforcement records, which could not be fetched.
