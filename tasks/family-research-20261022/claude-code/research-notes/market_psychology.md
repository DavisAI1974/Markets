# Psychology of Financial Markets: Behavioral Finance, Trader Psychology, and Their Footprints in Prices and Order Flow (with a futures/commodity lens)

Scope note for the report writer. This note is a HYPOTHESIS LAYER: for every behavioral idea it states (a) what is established, (b) what is contested or has failed replication, (c) the footprint the idea would leave in observable data (order flow, quotes, volume, timing), and (d) the competing mechanical/informational explanation that produces the SAME footprint. Most of the behavioral-finance evidence base is equities and retail brokerage accounts at daily-to-monthly horizons; the futures/professional evidence is thin and mostly from the open-outcry era (1995-1999 audit trails). Each finding below is tagged with asset class / participant type / horizon where the source allows. Items I could not source to a URL are listed under Gaps, not asserted. Research-environment note: several primary-source hosts (typeset.io, ideas.repec.org, faculty.haas.berkeley.edu, repository.cam.ac.uk) could not be fetched from this session's network, so some paper details rest on abstracts, digests and secondary summaries; those are flagged.

## Key Question 1: Core behavioral-finance findings (prospect theory/loss aversion, disposition, overconfidence/overtrading, attention, herding/cascades, anchoring/round numbers, extrapolation, behavioral momentum/reversal models, sentiment, feedback trading)

### Takeaway
The best-established individual-level facts are the disposition effect and the underperformance of high-turnover retail traders (both robust across countries and large account datasets); the weakest is "loss aversion" as a general, stake-independent psychological law, which a 2018 review argues is not supported by the evidence. The behavioral momentum/reversal models (BSV, DHS, Hong-Stein, DSSW) are internally coherent and produce rejectable predictions, but Fama's 1998 critique that over- and underreaction appear about equally often, and that anomalies are methodology-sensitive, has never been fully answered at the price level.

### Cited Findings
- Disposition effect (equities, retail, daily-to-annual). Shefrin and Statman (1985) named the tendency to sell winners and hold losers the "disposition effect"; Odean's large retail-brokerage study is described as taking it "from theoretical prediction to one of the most rigorously documented anomalies in finance," and a replication-tracking site judges it one of the few behavioral findings "not dismantled by replication failures" (advocacy-style source) — [Atticus Li replication hub](https://www.atticusli.com/replication-crisis/disposition-effect/). Weber and Camerer (1998) found it in the lab, and a later lab replication reported the effect but found gender may interfere with detection — [CAUSEweb replication submission](https://www.causeweb.org/usproc/sites/default/files/usresp/2016/submission.pdf).
- Overconfidence and overtrading (equities, US retail accounts, monthly). Barber and Odean argue overconfident investors trade more than rational investors and lower their expected utility; frequent trading reduces returns — [Barber & Odean, "The Courage of Misguided Convictions" (1999)](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers/Courage/Courage.pdf); the 2013 Handbook chapter "The Behavior of Individual Investors" summarizes overconfidence, disposition, attention and under-diversification as the four retail regularities — [EconPapers listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finchp:2-b-1533-1570); a survey notes individuals lose money on trades "even before frictions" — [Edmans, Gresham lecture notes](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2020-12-08-1800_EDMANS_InvestorMistakes_t.pdf).
- Attention-driven buying (equities, US retail, daily). Barber and Odean (2008) is cited for individual investors being net BUYERS of attention-grabbing stocks (news, abnormal volume, extreme returns) which subsequently underperform; the asymmetry is that attention drives buys, not sells, because retail rarely shorts — [Edmans notes](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2020-12-08-1800_EDMANS_InvestorMistakes_t.pdf); Da, Engelberg and Gao, "In Search of Attention," Journal of Finance 66(5) 1461-1499 (2011) is the Google-search-volume attention measure — [MPRA citation list](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/113620/1/MPRA_paper_113620.pdf).
- Extrapolative expectations (equities, survey respondents, quarterly-annual). Greenwood and Shleifer (RFS 2014) pool Gallup, Graham-Harvey, Shiller and Michigan surveys: expectations are extrapolative (rise with past returns), are strongly NEGATIVELY correlated with model-based expected returns, and are negatively correlated with subsequent realized returns — [NBER w18686](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18686/w18686.pdf); [RFS version](https://johanhombert.github.io/papers/Greenwood_Shleifer_RFS2014.pdf). Barberis, Greenwood, Jin and Shleifer's X-CAPM (JFE 2014/2015) embeds extrapolators alongside rational traders and notes Cochrane (2011) "finds the survey evidence uncomfortable, and recommends discarding it" — [BGJS JFE](https://blogs.cornell.edu/lawrencejin/files/2023/01/BGJS-JFE.pdf).
- Feedback trading / noise traders (theory). De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann (1990) model positive-feedback (momentum-chasing) noise traders; rational speculators who "jump on the bandwagon" early can INCREASE volatility around fundamentals; the model yields positive short-horizon return autocorrelation followed by reversal — [NBER w3250](https://www.nber.org/papers/w3250.pdf); [gwern mirror of the JF paper](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1990-delong.pdf).
- Behavioral momentum/reversal models (theory). Barberis-Shleifer-Vishny "A Model of Investor Sentiment" (JFE 1998) — [IDEAS listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v49y1998i3p307-343.html); Daniel-Hirshleifer-Subrahmanyam (1998) posit "a small number of cognitive biases" for a representative agent and ask whether they suffice to deliver short-horizon continuation and long-horizon reversal; both BSV and DHS assume prices are set by a single representative agent — [LSV Asset summary of the sentiment literature](https://www.lsvasset.com/pdf/research-papers/Investor-Sentiment.pdf); Hong and Stein (1999) use two boundedly rational groups ("newswatchers" and momentum traders) to unify underreaction, momentum trading and overreaction — [Hong-Stein working paper](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hrv:faseco:27693805).
- Herding and information cascades (theory). Banerjee (1992) and Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch (1992) show rational agents who observe predecessors' ACTIONS but not SIGNALS can rationally ignore their own signal; once a cascade starts "no further information is revealed," and cascades are fragile — a small influx of new information can dislodge them — [Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, Tamuz & Welch 2021 review](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2105.11044); [BHW IMF overview](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/staffp/2001/01/pdf/bikhchan.pdf). Scharfstein and Stein (1990) give the REPUTATIONAL mechanism: managers follow the herd "even when they believe that they could have made a better independent choice" — [UCL discovery paper](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/16558/1/16558.pdf). Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1992) launched the empirical institutional-herding literature — [same source](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/16558/1/16558.pdf).
- Anchoring / round-number clustering (equities, FX, futures; order-book level). Taiwan Futures Exchange data: investors submit a disproportionate share of LIMIT orders at round-number prices and those who do so perform worse, across futures and options — [Kuo, Lin & Zhao, "Cognitive Limitation and Investment Performance: Evidence from Limit Order Clustering" (EconBiz)](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/cognitive-limitation-and-investment-performance-evidence-from-limit-order-clustering/10013066819). Euronext stocks cluster at 5- and 10-cent increments, creating salient points where latent liquidity accumulates — [EFMA 2007 paper](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2007-Austria/papers/0286.pdf). In an FX order book, algorithmic traders place orders just ahead of prices ending in 0 or 5 to gain priority over the clusters — [Tick size reduction and price clustering in a FX order book (arXiv 1307.5440)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1307.5440). Equity stocks closing just above a round number reportedly outperform those just below by 24.6 bps next day (secondary summary) — [HKU paper](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/199292/1/content.pdf).
- Sentiment indices (equities, market-wide, monthly). Baker and Wurgler, "Investor Sentiment and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns" (NBER WP 10449, 2004; JF 2006) is the canonical composite-sentiment index; sentiment effects concentrate in hard-to-arbitrage stocks — [St. Gallen working paper citing it](https://ideas.repec.org/p/usg/sfwpfi/201712.html).
- Fama's critique. Fama (1998) argues "apparent overreaction to information is about as common as underreaction," consistent with chance under efficiency; most long-horizon anomalies "tend to disappear with reasonable changes in technique"; anomaly studies rarely test a SPECIFIC alternative; he credits behavioral models as "rejectable hypotheses" but calls the evidence "fragile" — [CFA Digest summary](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/cfa-digest/2000/02/market-efficiency-long-term-returns-and-behavioral-finance-digest-summary); [Chicago Booth Review](https://chicagobooth.edu/review/fair-game); [SSRN 15108](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.2139/ssrn.15108).
- Hirshleifer (2015), "Behavioral Finance," Annual Review of Financial Economics 7:133-159: reviews individual-level cognitive biases and argues the field should move to "social finance" (how financial ideas spread) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:anr:refeco:v:7:y:2015:p:133-159); [UCI PDF](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.uci.edu/dist/c/362/files/2020/07/behavioral-finance.pdf). The later "social transmission bias" program (Han, Hirshleifer, Walden) models how sociability and self-enhancing transmission determine the popularity and pricing of active strategies — [NBER w24281](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24281.pdf).

### Inferences
Footprint-vs-mechanical table for each core hypothesis (the behavioral hypothesis is the LEFT column, the competing mechanical/informational explanation the RIGHT):
- Disposition effect. Footprint: after a rally, elevated SELL-initiated flow from accounts in profit (take-profit clustering above entry prices) and sticky holding of losers (thin far-side offers on the way down, so declines meet little resting supply from longs). Mechanical twin: rational rebalancing / mean-reversion beliefs, tax-loss timing, margin-forced liquidation (which produces the OPPOSITE sign: losers sold), and dealer inventory management. Without account-level P&L you cannot distinguish them from the public tape; you only see the aggregate order imbalance.
- Overconfidence/overtrading. Footprint: high turnover by participants who subsequently lose; on public data this is unobservable except as "small-lot aggressive orders that are later adverse-selected." Mechanical twin: hedging, liquidity provision, noise from execution algos slicing parents into child orders.
- Attention. Footprint: a BUY-side skew in small-lot marketable orders right after news/volume spikes, with later reversal. Mechanical twin: news-driven information arrival (buys because the news was good), index/ETF rebalancing, options hedging flows. Distinguishing test: does the post-event drift revert (attention) or persist (information)?
- Extrapolation/feedback trading. Footprint: positive autocorrelation of signed order flow and short-horizon returns, followed by reversal; buyers arrive AFTER up-moves. Mechanical twin: order splitting of large parent orders (which also produces positively autocorrelated flow — see Q3), stop-loss/trailing-stop triggers, delta hedging of short gamma, CTA systematic rules (which are a mechanical implementation of extrapolation, not a psychological one).
- Herding/cascades. Footprint: synchronized same-side trades across many accounts within a short window, low dispersion of trade direction. Mechanical twin: common signal (everyone sees the same EIA number), shared algos/risk models, execution algorithms splitting orders. The tape cannot tell "I ignored my signal and copied" from "we all saw the same number."
- Round-number clustering. Footprint: resting limit-order depth concentrated at xx.00/xx.50 and stops just beyond; price "bounces" at round numbers and accelerates through them. Mechanical twin: tick-size and quoting conventions, options strikes at round numbers (dealer gamma pinning), and algorithms that deliberately quote one tick ahead of the cluster.
- Sentiment indices. Footprint: slow-moving (monthly) composite; at intraday horizons in commodities it is essentially unobservable and non-actionable.
- The theoretical models (BSV, DHS, Hong-Stein, DSSW) all predict the SAME aggregate footprint — continuation then reversal — so price data alone cannot identify WHICH psychology is at work, or whether any psychology is; this is the core of Fama's objection.

## Key Question 2: Evidence specific to futures and professional traders (Coval-Shumway; Locke-Mann; CTAs/trend followers; retail vs institutional in energy futures; Taiwan day traders)

### Takeaway
The only direct audit-trail evidence on futures professionals (CBOT T-bond locals 1998; CME floor locals mid-1990s) shows loss-averse risk-taking and disposition-type holding asymmetries, but with price effects that are small, transient and arbitraged away within minutes; herding studies using CFTC large-trader data find managed-money herding is real but does not destabilize prices. Day-trader studies (Taiwan equities) show aggregate losses and only a tiny persistent-winner tail; there is no comparable account-level study of retail energy-futures traders that I could find.

### Cited Findings
- Coval and Shumway (2005), "Do Behavioral Biases Affect Prices?", Journal of Finance 60(1):1-34. Asset: CBOT Treasury-bond futures, 1998; participants: 1,082 traders, 426 locals (market makers); data: 5 million+ transactions, audit trail; horizon: intraday (morning vs afternoon). Findings: traders "appear strongly loss-averse"; those with morning losses are about 16% more likely to take above-average afternoon risk than those with morning gains (an earlier draft says about 15%); losing traders actively BUY at higher prices and SELL at lower prices than prevailed; prices set by loss-averse traders are "reversed significantly more quickly than those set by unbiased traders"; short-term afternoon volatility rises with the prevalence of morning losses among locals but overall afternoon volatility does not; other participants treat them as noise traders and "trade aggressively against them," so the price impact is temporary — [CFA Digest summary](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2005/11/do-behavioral-biases-affect-prices-digest-summary); [EconPapers listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:60:y:2005:i:1:p:1-34); [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9284); [2003 working draft](https://typeset.io/pdf/do-behavioral-biases-affect-prices-42pcrh1of7.pdf).
- Locke and Mann (2005), "Professional trader discipline and trade disposition," Journal of Financial Economics 76(2):401-444, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.01.004. Asset: CME currency and agricultural futures pits; participants: professional floor traders (locals). The paper frames "discipline" as strategies professionals use to minimize behavioral influences and tests whether professionals avoid the retail disposition pattern — [IDEAS listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v76y2005i2p401-444.html); secondary sources report that traders held losses significantly longer than gains but the authors found no measurable cost in their sample (commercial blog; verify) — [Trader's Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/why-traders-cut-winners-hold-losers); a claim that median loss holding time was over four times gain holding time is attributed to "Lock and Mann" by a broker blog and is UNCONFIRMED — [IG](https://ig.com/sg/master-your-trading-mind/your-trading-dna/is-your-personality-affecting-your-trading). A related study, "Do Losses Linger?", examines whether prior losses affect subsequent professional trading — [Nuffield (Murphy)](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Users/MurphyA/Do%20Losses%20Linger.pdf).
- Haigh and List (2005), "Do Professional Traders Exhibit Myopic Loss Aversion? An Experimental Analysis," Journal of Finance 60(1):523-534: CBOT professionals in a lab experiment — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v60y2005i1p523-534.html) (results not retrieved here; see Gaps).
- Feng and Seasholes (2005), Review of Finance: sophistication and trading experience attenuate (but do not eliminate) the disposition effect (Chinese brokerage data) — [PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/feng_seasholes_RoF_2005.pdf) (details not extracted).
- Herding among large futures traders. Boyd, Buyuksahin, Haigh and Harris (2016), Journal of Futures Markets 36(7):671-694: thirty US futures markets, 2004-2009, CFTC large-trader data; "significant herding levels" within managed-money (hedge-fund) traders; "little evidence that herding by managed money traders serves to destabilize prices"; herding is positively associated with number of traders and floor-based markets, negatively with volume and electronic trading; more public information reduces herding — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jfutmk:v:36:y:2016:i:7:p:671-694); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v36y2016i7p671-694.html). Brunetti, Buyuksahin and Harris (2013), Energy Journal 34(3):83-104, find little evidence that speculator herding destabilizes crude-oil futures — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:enejou:v:34:y:2013:i:3:p:83-104); related Fed FEDS paper — [Federal Reserve 2011-57](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2011/201157/index.html).
- CTAs/trend followers and price impact. CFTC-position study: trading by large hedge funds and CTAs "is influenced in small part by past price changes, but is not driven by them" — [farmdoc / Illinois](https://farmdoc.illinois.edu/publications/the-effects-of-futures-trading-by-large-hedge-funds-and-ctas-on-market-volatility). Caveat: the CFTC CTA/CPO classification is by legal registration status and "encompasses most hedge funds," so it is not a clean trend-follower proxy — [McMaster ESR article](https://prod-mulpress.lib.mcmaster.ca/esr/article/view/538/516). Kang, Rouwenhorst and Tang: commodity futures prices predictably rise (fall) after HEDGERS' buying (selling); speculators act as impatient liquidity consumers rather than pure trend followers — [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69870/f69870.pdf). A 2026 preprint argues trend signals trigger directional trades whose impact reinforces the signal (self-reinforcing loop); not peer reviewed — [alphaxiv 2607.01550](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.01550.md). Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen (JPM 2017) document positive trend-following returns across 67 markets back to 1880 — [AQR PDF](https://www.TrendFollowing.com/whitepaper/Century_Evidence_Trend_Following.pdf) (AQR-produced; no independent replication found).
- Index speculation / "financialization" (commodities, 2006-2009). Irwin and Sanders: "the lack of a direct empirical link between index fund trading and commodity futures prices casts considerable doubt" on the index-bubble view; swap-dealer positions did not predict returns in 14 commodities 2006-2009, though an extended oil sample back to 1993 showed some predictability — [Irwin-Sanders (Scribd copy of AEPP paper)](https://www.scribd.com/document/699691174/aepp-2Fppq032). Kesicky (2010) finds speculators' impact in the 2008 oil spike small and short-term; Hamilton (2009) doubted speculation caused it — [McMaster ESR](https://prod-mulpress.lib.mcmaster.ca/esr/article/view/538/516).
- Day traders (Taiwan Stock Exchange equities, 1992-2006, all day traders). Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean (and Zhang in the published version, "Learning, Fast or Slow," Review of Asset Pricing Studies 10(1):61-93, 2020): aggregate day-trader performance is negative; 74% of day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses; 97% of day traders are likely to lose money in future day trading; 80% quit within two years; the authors conclude rational-learning models do not explain speculative trading — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rasset:v:10:y:2020:i:1:p:61-93.); [working paper](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/day%20traders/day%20trading%20and%20learning%20110217.pdf). Related JFM 2014 paper: less than 1% of the day-trader population predictably earns positive abnormal returns net of fees — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24); the 2004 version reported over 80% lose after transaction costs — [CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/individual-investing/do-day-traders-make-money/).
- Retail access to energy futures. CME Micro WTI (1/10 size, 100 barrels) launched July 2021 and traded over 1 million contracts in its first month (exchange's own figure) — [CME OpenMarkets](https://cmegroup.com/openmarkets/micros.html); I found no academic study of retail behavior in micro energy futures.

### Inferences
- Coval-Shumway is the single most relevant result for an intraday NG/CL program: it says a behavioral bias (loss-chasing by market makers after losses) DOES show up on the tape — as aggressive buys above / sells below prevailing prices in the afternoon — but is reverted quickly because others fade it. Footprint: post-loss (afternoon) elevated aggressive small-lot flow with fast reversal, higher short-horizon volatility but not higher session volatility. Mechanical twin: inventory-constrained market makers forced to unwind (risk limits) produce identical aggressive flow with reversal — the audit trail (P&L per account) was needed to attribute it to loss aversion; the public tape cannot.
- The practical reading of Coval-Shumway for a lag strategy: afternoon reversion after loss-driven aggressive prints is a candidate fade signal, but it was measured in 1998 open-outcry T-bonds; whether it survives in electronic CL/NG is untested (Gap).
- Locke-Mann's "discipline" framing is directly usable as a measurable variable (relative holding time of losing vs winning trades) if account-level data existed; on public data it is unobservable. Its mechanical twin: locals hold losers longer because they are PROVIDING liquidity against order flow and expect mean reversion — a rational inventory strategy with the same holding-time signature.
- CFTC-based herding evidence is weekly and position-level, far from intraday; it says managed money moves together but not that it moves prices. The order-splitting literature (Q3) implies that even if CTAs herd, their execution algos would leave an autocorrelated-flow footprint indistinguishable from a single large informed order.
- CTAs are the cleanest case where "extrapolative behavior" is MECHANICAL by construction: trend-following rules are deterministic functions of past prices. Their footprint (buying after up-moves at predictable lookback breaks, e.g., 20/50/100-day channels, with stops) is observable in principle at daily horizons and partly intraday around settlement, but is not psychology.
- The Taiwan result (97% lose) is equities and retail; the mechanism it supports — persistent overconfident participation funded by a few winners — likely transfers to retail futures, but there is no account-level energy-futures evidence to confirm it.

### Gaps
- Locke and Mann's exact numbers (sample size, holding-time ratio, the discipline-success correlation) could not be fetched; secondary quotes conflict and are from commercial blogs.
- Haigh and List (2005) results on myopic loss aversion in CBOT professionals not extracted (the paper is in the search results but content not retrieved).
- No peer-reviewed study found on retail vs institutional behavior in energy futures specifically (Micro WTI/NG, or CFTC "nonreportable" positions as a retail proxy).
- No evidence found on whether Coval-Shumway's afternoon-loss effect replicates in electronic markets or in energy futures.
- Trend-follower price-impact evidence remains contested; the 2026 "Is Trend Still Your Friend?" preprint is unreviewed.

## Key Question 3: Critiques and limits (replication crisis; adaptive markets; mechanical explanations for "behavioral" footprints; Fama; does retail psychology matter in algorithmic markets)

### Takeaway
Loss aversion as a general law is the most damaged pillar (Gal and Rucker 2018), while the disposition effect and retail overtrading have held up; Lo's adaptive markets hypothesis reframes biases as context-dependent heuristics rather than fixed errors. Most important for tape analysis: the microstructure literature shows that the long memory in order flow — the most "herd-like" footprint in the data — is predominantly produced by ORDER SPLITTING by individual institutions, not by many traders copying each other, so herding footprints at intraday horizons are usually mechanical.

### Cited Findings
- Loss aversion contested. Gal and Rucker (2018), "The Loss of Loss Aversion," Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3):497-516: "current evidence does not support that losses, on balance, tend to be any more impactful than gains"; large financial losses can matter more without a loss-aversion explanation — [SSRN 3049660](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3049660); a 2020 rebuttal (Mrkva et al., JCP) argues loss aversion has moderators and reports of its death are exaggerated — [Jason Collins, "The case against loss aversion"](https://www.jasoncollins.blog/posts/the-case-against-loss-aversion). Yechiam argues Galanter and Pliner (1974) were wrongly cited as showing loss aversion — [Atticus Li, loss aversion](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/loss-aversion/). Counter-position: Chicago Booth authors (promoting their book) claim endowment effect, hyperbolic discounting, social preferences and loss aversion "are robust to replication attempts" — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/how-scrutiny-iteration-made-behavioral-economics-better). Popular critiques ("The death of behavioral economics," Hreha) and the Astral Codex Ten response summarize the debate — [ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/on-hreha-on-behavioral-economics).
- Myopic loss aversion under retest: a preregistered stress test (Schwaiger, Strucks, Zeisberger 2024) of narrow bracketing + loss aversion and risk-taking — [Netspar paper](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/AP_2025_024_Goossens.pdf) (results not visible in snippet).
- Adaptive Markets Hypothesis. Lo (2004), Journal of Portfolio Management 30:15-29: reconciles efficiency with behavioral alternatives via evolutionary principles; extends Simon's satisficing; loss aversion and overconfidence are adaptive heuristics that can be maladaptive when the environment changes; implications include time-varying risk premia and arbitrage opportunities that appear and disappear — [MIT abstract page](https://web.mit.edu/~alo/www/Papers/JPM2004.html).
- Fama (1998): see Q1 — overreaction about as frequent as underreaction; anomalies methodology-sensitive; "fragile" evidence — [CFA Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/cfa-digest/2000/02/market-efficiency-long-term-returns-and-behavioral-finance-digest-summary).
- Order splitting, not herding, drives long memory in order flow. Market-order signs are a long-memory process (power-law autocorrelation; exponent near 0.5, Hurst about 0.75) — [Lillo & Farmer / Bouchaud et al. line, arXiv physics/0509194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0509194); Toth et al. (2015) decompose the autocorrelation into same-trader and different-trader parts using broker-labelled data and find order SPLITTING is the main driver; Gerig's LSE study found cross-broker correlations decay quickly while individual brokers' flow has long memory; "at intraday horizons, the persistence is overwhelmingly due to splitting rather than herding" — [Bouchaud et al., "Order flow and price formation" (arXiv 2105.00521)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00521); the Lillo-Mike-Farmer model derives long memory from power-law-sized hidden (meta)orders executed in pieces, with the autocorrelation exponent tied to the metaorder size exponent — [arXiv 2301.13505](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.13505). Long memory of order flow is also documented in FX spot — [arXiv 1504.04354](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04354). Dissent: Axioglou and Skouras argue the apparent long memory is largely a nonstationarity artifact — [arXiv 2105.00521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00521).
- Herding is REDUCED by electronic trading and volume and increased in floor-based markets — the Boyd et al. (2016) result in Q2 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jfutmk:v:36:y:2016:i:7:p:671-694).
- Algorithms exploit the round-number cluster rather than form it: in FX order books, algorithmic traders place orders just above/below prices ending in 0 or 5 to take priority over the clusters — [arXiv 1307.5440](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1307.5440).
- Flash crash (E-mini S&P, May 6 2010, CFTC audit trail). Published version (Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi, Tuzun, JF 72(3):967-998, 2017): the most active intraday intermediaries (HFTs) "did not change their trading pattern when prices fell" — [Cambridge repository abstract](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/304244); earlier CFTC working paper: HFTs did not cause the crash but contributed by "demanding immediacy ahead of other participants," aggressively removing the last contracts at the best bid/ask and amplifying a short-lived liquidity imbalance — [CFTC OCE paper](https://www.cftc.gov/idc/groups/public/@economicanalysis/documents/file/oce_flashcrash0314.pdf); CFTC presentation: HFT behavior on May 6 was unchanged from prior days — small inventory, high volume, taking more liquidity than providing — [Columbia CAP slides](https://cap.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/Documents/11-1910/The%20Flash%20Crash-%20The%20Impact%20of%20High%20Frequency%20Trading%20on%20an%20Electronic%20Market.pdf).
- Coval-Shumway's own conclusion that other traders arbitrage away the loss-averse locals' price impact is itself evidence that psychology's price footprint in futures is transient — [CFA Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2005/11/do-behavioral-biases-affect-prices-digest-summary).

### Inferences
- The decisive methodological point for tape work: the three most-cited "crowd psychology" footprints — autocorrelated same-side flow (herding/feedback), cascading sells through a level (panic), and accelerations past round numbers (stop-runs) — each have a fully mechanical generator (order splitting; stop orders converting to market orders; algos quoting ahead of clusters). The behavioral and mechanical hypotheses are observationally equivalent on anonymous tape; they separate only with participant identifiers (audit trail) or with event-timing tests (e.g., does the flow respond to a public release identically across accounts?).
- Adaptive-markets framing turns "is the bias real?" into "under what regime does a given footprint predict?" — which is congruent with per-cell evaluation (regime x event x side) rather than pooled tests.
- "Does retail psychology matter in algo-dominated markets?" The evidence here says: individual biases (Coval-Shumway) leave transient footprints that liquidity providers fade; herding declines with electronic trading (Boyd et al.); flash-crash HFTs behaved mechanically, not emotionally. The remaining channels through which psychology could matter in CL/NG intraday are (i) the parent-order decisions of humans that algos then execute (unobservable except via the splitting signature), (ii) stop placement (a human choice that becomes a mechanical trigger), and (iii) slow-moving sentiment/positioning (weekly COT), none of which is a clean intraday nowcast.
- Nothing in the replication debate overturns the disposition effect or retail overtrading; what has been weakened is the quantitative universality of loss aversion (lambda about 2) and the cleanliness of the prospect-theory micro-foundation.

### Gaps
- Camerer et al. (2016, Science) replication of 18 experimental-economics papers (background knowledge: 11 of 18 replicated) was not confirmed by any retrieved source.
- Lo's 2017 book "Adaptive Markets" was not directly sourced; only the 2004 JPM paper.
- No source found that directly tests "panic footprint = stop orders" with order-type-labelled futures data; the stop-cascade mechanism is well described only for FX (Osler, Q4).
- The published JF 2017 flash-crash text ("hot potato" volume statistics) could not be fetched; the "hot potato" characterization rests on the working paper and should be attributed to it, not the published version.

## Key Question 4: Market-wide phenomena attributed to psychology (bubbles/crashes, volatility clustering, panics/liquidity withdrawal, capitulation, short squeezes, round-number support/resistance, stop hunting, self-fulfilling technical levels)

### Takeaway
The best-documented of these are mechanical-with-a-psychological-origin: stop-loss and take-profit orders cluster at and just beyond round numbers (Osler 2003, FX), which makes support/resistance bounces and post-break accelerations self-fulfilling regardless of belief; technical analysis is used heavily by professionals precisely because they believe prices carry psychological/non-fundamental influences (Menkhoff 2010; Menkhoff-Taylor 2007). Volatility clustering has two live explanations (news-arrival mixture vs interacting heterogeneous agents) that are not mutually exclusive, and "capitulation" has no peer-reviewed support as a bottom signal.

### Cited Findings
- Stop-loss and take-profit clustering (FX; a large dealing bank's customer orders; ~9,700 orders, Sep 1999-Apr 2000). Osler (2003), Journal of Finance 58(5):1791-1819: TAKE-PROFIT orders cluster strongly AT round numbers (explaining why trends reverse at support/resistance), STOP-LOSS orders cluster just BEYOND round numbers (explaining why trends accelerate after breaking them) — [NY Fed Staff Report 125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.html); [PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf); [EconPapers JF listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:58:y:2003:i:5:p:1791-1819).
- Technical analysis among professionals (fund managers, 5 countries, n=692). Menkhoff (2010), JBF 34(11):2573-2586: most managers rely on TA; at a horizon of WEEKS TA is the most important form of analysis; technicians are as experienced/educated/successful/overconfident as others; TA is somewhat more common in smaller firms; its use correlates most strongly with the belief that "prices are heavily determined by psychological influences," and technicians apply trend-following behavior — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:34:y:2010:i:11:p:2573-2586); [Hannover DP 446](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:han:dpaper:dp-446). Menkhoff and Taylor (2007), JEL 45(4):936-972: TA is common in FX and may be profitable; of four explanations (irrationality, exploiting central-bank intervention, efficient information processing, informing on non-fundamental influences) the last is most plausible — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:45:y:2007:i:4:p:936-972); [Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/30672/).
- Round-number limit-order clustering and its exploitation by algos: see Q1 (Taiwan futures/options; Euronext; FX order book) — [EconBiz](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/cognitive-limitation-and-investment-performance-evidence-from-limit-order-clustering/10013066819); [arXiv 1307.5440](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1307.5440).
- Stop hunting in futures: a practitioner article concedes it "found no study measuring round-number stop clustering in CME futures," so applying Osler's FX pattern to index futures "is an inference, not a measured result" — [TradersPost blog](https://blog.traderspost.io/article/stop-loss-hunting-detection); other practitioner sites describe stop orders converting to market orders and firing simultaneously once a cluster level trades (plausible mechanism, not proof of deliberate hunting) — [NexusFi](https://nexusfi.com/a/market-structure/liquidity-sweeps-stop-hunts); [Thor Trade Copier](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/is-stop-hunting-real).
- Volatility clustering. Two competing hypotheses: the "news arrival process" (exogenous) vs emergent dynamics of interacting heterogeneous agents — [Alfarano, Lux & Wagner](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/EmpValidACE.AlfaranoWagnerLux.pdf); Andersen-Bollerslev (1997) mixture-of-distributions with many heterogeneous short-lived information arrivals producing long-run dependence — [typeset PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/heterogeneous-information-arrivals-and-return-volatility-1fyvkn76gr.pdf); agent-based models reproduce fat tails and clustering from interaction alone, typically via switching between low/high-activity regimes with heavy-tailed durations (Cont 2007 review) — [Alfarano et al. slides](https://indico.ijclab.in2p3.fr/event/3099/attachments/6779/8017/Alfarano_et_al_05_-_computecs.pdf); [Cont, arXiv cond-mat/0407533](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0407533v2); an order-book heterogeneous-agent model — [arXiv 1601.00229](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.00229).
- Panics / liquidity withdrawal: flash crash findings in Q3 — HFTs "demanding immediacy ahead of other participants" amplified a short-lived liquidity imbalance (working paper), published version stresses HFT behavior did not change — [CFTC OCE](https://www.cftc.gov/idc/groups/public/@economicanalysis/documents/file/oce_flashcrash0314.pdf); [Cambridge abstract](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/304244).
- Short squeezes (equities, US and EU). Allen, Haas, Pirovano and Tengulov (2023): stock-day squeeze events are "rare and short-lived," but about 25% of unique stocks experience one in a given year in both US and EU; market squeezes "impair price discovery" — [Harvard Law Forum summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2023/09/28/how-prevalent-are-short-squeezes-evidence-from-the-us-and-europe/); [SFI research paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2363.html). Xu and Liu (2012, via CXO): 26,343 events 1995-2009, trigger = one-day return >= 15%, price keeps climbing about 1.5 days to a 27% peak before reversing; impact rises with short interest — [CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/short-selling/short-squeeze-timeline). 2021 social-platform squeezes impeded market quality of the stocks and their competitors, with options central — [USI paper](https://feeds.usi.ch/documents/attachment/2671/squeezing-shorts-through-social-news-platforms.pdf).
- Capitulation: no peer-reviewed test found; sources are practitioner/crypto blogs that concede capitulation "is only confirmed in hindsight" and that move intensity alone does not confirm a bottom — [Glassnode](https://research.glassnode.com/cost-basis-distribution-capitulation-metrics/); [Technical Analysis of Stocks & Commodities](https://technical.traders.com/tradersonline/display.asp?art=6291).
- Bubbles and positive feedback: DSSW (1990) is the canonical model (Q1) — [NBER w3250](https://www.nber.org/papers/w3250.pdf); for commodities the 2008 oil-spike debate concluded speculation's impact was small/short-term (Kesicky; Hamilton) — [McMaster ESR](https://prod-mulpress.lib.mcmaster.ca/esr/article/view/538/516).

### Inferences
- Osler's result is the cleanest bridge between psychology and mechanism: round-number preference is a cognitive habit (why people put orders there), but once the orders rest, the price dynamics are mechanical and PREDICTABLE from the order book: expect resting depth (take-profits = limit orders) AT round numbers and a thin zone with latent market-order supply JUST BEYOND (stops). Footprint in MBP-10/tape: (a) depth build-up at xx.00/xx.50; (b) on a break, a burst of aggressive same-side trades with widening spread and depth depletion several ticks past the level; (c) fast partial retrace once the stop cluster is exhausted. Mechanical twin: options-strike gamma hedging at round strikes and algos quoting one tick ahead; both produce (a). The stop-burst (b) is distinguishable from informed flow by its timing (immediately upon the level trading, not upon news) and by the retrace (c).
- "Stop hunting" as a deliberate act is unproven; what IS supported is that stops cluster and that any sufficiently large order (informed or not) will trigger them, so the hunting footprint and the information footprint coincide.
- Technical levels are self-fulfilling to the degree that professionals act on them (Menkhoff: TA dominant at weekly horizons), which means the level effect should scale with how widely observed the level is; prior-day high/low, settlement, and round numbers are strong candidates in CL/NG, but none has been measured with order-type data.
- For volatility clustering in energy futures the news-arrival explanation has an obvious concrete form (scheduled EIA/weather releases); the heterogeneous-agent explanation predicts clustering even in the ABSENCE of news. A test: compare clustering persistence on release vs non-release days.
- Short-squeeze evidence is equities; in futures the analogue is forced covering of margin-constrained shorts (or longs) — a mechanical liquidation with the same accelerating one-sided footprint; no behavioral content is needed.

### Gaps
- No study found measuring stop/take-profit clustering in CME energy futures; Osler's data are a single FX bank's customer orders in 1999-2000.
- No peer-reviewed study found on "capitulation" volume signatures marking bottoms in any asset class.
- Full text of Osler (2003) not fetched; details beyond the abstract are unverified.
- No decisive empirical test found adjudicating news-arrival vs heterogeneous-agent explanations of volatility clustering.

## Key Question 5: Neuroscience and physiology (Coates-Herbert cortisol/testosterone; Lo-Repin physiological responses; Cueva et al.) — what they do and do not establish about prices

### Takeaway
Physiological studies establish that traders' bodies respond to risk and P&L (cortisol tracks uncertainty, testosterone tracks daily profits, autonomic arousal spikes around volatility), and lab administration of these hormones shifts individuals toward risk; none of them measures a price effect in a real market, samples are tiny (10-17 traders), and the leap from individual hormones to market bubbles is the authors' speculation.

### Cited Findings
- Coates and Herbert (2008), PNAS, "Endogenous steroids and financial risk taking on a London trading floor": 17 male City traders, 8 business days, saliva sampled twice daily with P&L logged; a trader's morning testosterone predicted that day's profitability; testosterone was higher on days traders beat their monthly average; cortisol did NOT spike after losses but tracked RISK/uncertainty (matched options-implied uncertainty); the authors speculate sustained cortisol could raise risk aversion in crashes and high testosterone could amplify risk-taking in bubbles, and frame results modestly — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2329689); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18413617/); [Neuroanthropology critique](https://neuroanthropology.net/2008/04/17/testosterone-and-cortisol-explain-market-behaviour/).
- Lo and Repin (2002), Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience 14(3):323-339: 10 professional traders wired for real-time physiological signals during live trading; statistically significant electrodermal responses during market events vs control periods and cardiovascular changes during high-volatility periods; responses varied with experience — [MIT abstract page](https://web.mit.edu/Alo/www/Papers/repin.html); [NBER w8508](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8508.pdf); [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/mar02/measuring-stress-financial-traders).
- Cueva et al. (2015), Scientific Reports, "Cortisol and testosterone increase financial risk taking and may destabilize markets": first measured salivary hormones in an experimental asset market, then ADMINISTERED cortisol (n=34) or testosterone (n=41) to young men before a trading game; both shifted investment toward riskier assets; cortisol appeared to change risk preferences directly, testosterone to raise optimism about future prices; endogenous cortisol predicted later risk-taking and price instability in the lab market — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4489095); [Imperial Spiral](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/f7c8d3d5-c530-47ca-b4b8-6c01d406131b). Related: Danese et al. (2017), testosterone and cortisol in an ambiguity task with 78 male students — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2017.00068/pdf); a multi-study testosterone investigation — [peeref](https://www.peeref.com/works/22513933) (results not extracted).

### Inferences
- What these establish: a plausible biological channel for Coval-Shumway's "take more risk after losses" and for risk-appetite cycles; cortisol's tracking of UNCERTAINTY rather than losses is the most market-relevant detail, because uncertainty (implied vol) is observable while hormone levels are not.
- What they do not establish: any identified price footprint. No study links a trading floor's hormone levels to subsequent order flow or returns; the "bubbles/crashes" implication is conjecture. Any footprint would be indirect (more aggressive flow in high-vol regimes), and its mechanical twin — risk-limit/VaR-driven deleveraging in high vol — is identical on the tape.
- Sample sizes (10-17 traders; 34-41 lab subjects) and single-sex samples mean none of this should be weighted as established at the market level.

### Gaps
- No replication of Coates-Herbert on a trading floor found; Cueva (2015) is a lab study with Coates as co-author, not an independent replication.
- Coates's 2012 book "The Hour Between Dog and Wolf" not sourced.
- No study found linking physiological measures to futures-market order flow.

## Key Question 6: Newer directions (narrative economics; social media/attention and GameStop; expectations surveys; ML sentiment) and intraday predictive power in commodity futures

### Takeaway
Social-media attention demonstrably coincides with 30-minute-ahead volume and volatility (GameStop), but studies disagree on whether it transmits through measured retail order imbalances or carries information; news-sentiment ML models show in-sample explanatory power for crude-oil returns at daily/weekly horizons; I found NO study demonstrating intraday predictive power of sentiment for natural gas futures, and the oil work does not report out-of-sample high-frequency trading profitability.

### Cited Findings
- Narrative economics. Shiller (2017), AER 107(4):967-1004 (presidential address): contagious narratives induce aggregate shifts in beliefs and drive macroeconomic events — [NBER w23075](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23075); a 2024 NBER paper operationalizes it — [NBER w32602](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32602/w32602.pdf).
- GameStop / Reddit (equities, retail, 2021, intraday). Reddit comment activity has a significant positive link with trading measures in the FOLLOWING 30 minutes (attention mechanism) with little sign the posts carried information about future prices; NO significant relationship with marketable retail order imbalances, and the authors could not separate retail from other trades — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35371372/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965552); a Springer paper reports similar and calls the evidence contested — [Financial Markets and Portfolio Management](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11408-022-00407-w.pdf). WSB tone was positively associated with future GME returns, volatility, spreads and volume, with 462 influential users having disproportionate effect — [IIMB WP 644](https://www.iimb.ac.in/sites/default/files/2021-12/WP%20No.%20644.pdf). ML on Reddit argues coordinated activity drove GME/BB/AMC/NOK surges — [arXiv 2401.14999](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.14999.pdf); a hype-cycle model — [MPRA 112734](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/112734/1/MPRA_paper_112734.pdf); brokerage-account study on positioning and order flow — [Paderborn TAF WP 58](https://wiwi.uni-paderborn.de/fileadmin-wiwi/cetar/TAF_Working_Paper_Series/TAF_WP_058_HassoMuellerPelsterWarkulat2021_rev.pdf).
- Expectations surveys: Greenwood-Shleifer extrapolation evidence (Q1) — [NBER w18686](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18686/w18686.pdf).
- ML sentiment and crude oil (daily/weekly). Li et al. (2021), Energy Economics 95: news-headline sentiment + bidirectional LSTM; "the shock of news sentiment significantly causes the fluctuation of oil futures prices"; outperforms econometric/ML baselines — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v95y2021ics0140988321000451.html). Jung and Kim (2025): ML-extracted news sentiment "is significantly effective in explaining the crude oil futures returns" and also explains changes in trader-group positions — [SeoulTech](https://pure.seoultech.ac.kr/en/publications/predicting-crude-oil-returns-and-trading-position-evidence-from-n/). CrudeBERT (2023): sentiment methods "still play only a minor role in forecasting crude oil prices" — [arXiv 2305.06140](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06140). Dai et al. (2026 preprint): GPT-4o + FinBERT multi-dimensional sentiment for WEEKLY WTI returns; intensity and uncertainty features most important; not peer reviewed — [arXiv 2603.11408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11408). Essex preprint: news predicting direction of Brent realized volatility built from intraday returns (closest to intraday; results not seen) — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/43805/1/Can_News_Predict_the_Direction_of_Oil_Price_Volatility__A_Language_Model_Approach_with_SHAP_Explanations__review_2_.pdf).
- Attention measure for stocks: Da, Engelberg, Gao (2011) Google SVI — [citation](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/113620/1/MPRA_paper_113620.pdf).

### Inferences
- The GameStop literature's most useful lesson for tape work is a negative one: even in the most retail-driven episode on record, attention's link to MEASURED retail order imbalance was not significant; attention showed up as volume/volatility, not as a clean directional retail imbalance. Footprint of attention: volume and volatility spikes after social/news bursts, with weak or unstable direction. Mechanical twin: the news itself (information) and the options/hedging flows it triggers.
- For NG/CL intraday, the sentiment literature is at the wrong horizon (daily/weekly) and mostly in-sample; the scheduled-release structure (EIA storage, weather model runs) means "news arrival" is largely KNOWN-TIME, so an information/mechanical explanation (positioning into a known release, then repricing) dominates any sentiment story at intraday horizons.
- Narrative economics is a long-horizon hypothesis with no intraday footprint; it could only enter as a slow regime variable.

### Gaps
- No study found on intraday sentiment/attention predictive power for natural gas futures; none reporting out-of-sample intraday trading profitability for crude oil.
- Shiller's book-length "Narrative Economics" (2019) not sourced; only the AER address.
- No source found on social-media attention effects in commodity futures (e.g., retail "oil to zero" April 2020 episode in USO/CL).

## Cross-cutting summary: observable vs unobservable, with mechanical twins (for the report writer)

### Takeaway
On an anonymous futures tape every behavioral footprint has an observationally equivalent mechanical generator; attribution to psychology has only ever been achieved with participant-identified audit-trail data (Coval-Shumway, Locke-Mann, Kirilenko et al., Osler's bank orders, Taiwan account data). The behavioral layer should therefore be treated as a set of named alternatives whose predictions about TIMING and REVERSAL (fast reversion after loss-driven aggression; stop bursts immediately upon level-trades then retrace; attention volume without direction) can be tested per cell, not as a source of direction on its own.

### Cited Findings
- Behavioral price effects in futures were transient and arbitraged (Coval-Shumway) — [CFA Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2005/11/do-behavioral-biases-affect-prices-digest-summary).
- Flow autocorrelation is predominantly order splitting, not herding (Toth et al.; Bouchaud et al.) — [arXiv 2105.00521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00521).
- Stop/take-profit clustering makes technical levels self-fulfilling mechanically (Osler) — [NY Fed SR125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf).
- Managed-money herding exists but does not destabilize futures prices and declines with electronic trading (Boyd et al.) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jfutmk:v:36:y:2016:i:7:p:671-694).
- Professionals use TA most at weekly horizons and because they believe prices are psychologically driven (Menkhoff) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:34:y:2010:i:11:p:2573-2586).

### Inferences
- Observable on MBP-10/tape: depth concentration at round numbers; aggressive-flow bursts and depth depletion just beyond levels; signed-flow autocorrelation; post-event volume/volatility; fast reversal vs persistence after aggressive prints. Unobservable: which account is in profit or loss, holding times, whether a trader ignored a private signal, hormone states, beliefs. Everything in the second list requires the exchange audit trail or account data.
- A disciplined use of this layer: for each mechanical finding in the NG program (e.g., imbalance -> direction; far-side recruitment at turning points), write the behavioral alternative beside it (e.g., "loss-chasing locals" vs "inventory unwinding"; "stop cluster" vs "informed sweep") and specify the TIMING/REVERSAL prediction that separates them, then test per cell.
